## Q1. Does India have a dedicated trade secrets statute as of September 2026?

### Takeaway
**No.** As of the most recent evidence I could obtain, India has **no dedicated trade secrets statute**. The 22nd Law Commission of India issued its **289th Report, "Trade Secrets and Economic Espionage," on 5 March 2024**, annexing a draft **Protection of Trade Secrets Bill, 2024**. That Bill has **never been introduced in Parliament** — it remains a Law Commission recommendation with no legislative follow-through. This is the area in flux: a Law Commission draft sitting un-introduced for two and a half years is not a Bill on the cusp of passage, but it is also the most concrete legislative proposal India has ever had on the subject, so it could move.

- Notable contents of the draft Bill as reported: an explanation that **"experiences and skills" acquired during "normal professional practice" do not qualify as trade secrets**; a carve-out that acquisition/use/disclosure is **not "misappropriation" when done to expose illegal activity or protect the public interest**; and a **compulsory-licensing section** allowing the Government to license a trade secret in a public emergency involving substantial public interest. — [Intepat, "The Trade Secret Bill, 2024: A Comprehensive Analysis"](https://www.intepat.com/blog/the-trade-secret-bill-2024-a-comprehensive-analysis); [IIPRD, "The Proposed Trade Secrets Bill, 2024"](https://www.iiprd.com/the-proposed-trade-secrets-bill-2024-legal-framework-and-economic-impact/) (both search summaries).

### Inferences
- **Practical consequence for the founder:** do not build strategy around a statute arriving. Plan on the contract-plus-equity regime persisting for the whole of the product's first commercial cycle. If the Bill *does* pass, it would improve the position (clearer definition, a defined misappropriation tort, possibly better interim relief), so a programme built for today's law is not wasted — it is the same evidentiary groundwork the Bill would require ("reasonable steps to keep it secret" is the universal precondition).
- The compulsory-licensing clause in the draft is worth noting but is not a realistic risk for a room-lighting controller. It matters for pharma/agri.
- The "experiences and skills" carve-out in the draft mirrors what Indian courts *already* do (see *Ambiance India*, Q2) — i.e. the Bill largely codifies existing judicial practice on the employment side rather than changing it. So the employment-mobility risk profile would not shift much.

## Q2. How are trade secrets actually protected in India today?

### Takeaway
Through **four overlapping mechanisms, none of them a trade secrets statute**: (1) **contract** — NDAs and employment confidentiality clauses, enforceable; (2) the **equitable doctrine of breach of confidence**, which operates even without a contract; (3) **Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act 1872**, which is a *constraint* not a protection — it voids agreements in restraint of trade, with the critical consequence that **post-employment non-competes are generally unenforceable in India while non-disclosure obligations are enforceable**; and (4) the **Specific Relief Act 1963**, which supplies the injunction machinery. Remedies realistically obtainable are interim and permanent injunctions, delivery-up/return of materials, and damages; **injunctions are the real remedy and damages are rarely a meaningful recovery.** Litigation is slow.

### Cited Findings

**No statute; common law + contract + equity.**
- "Courts in the country have gradually evolved a common law framework grounded in principles of equity, contract, and breach of confidence to safeguard proprietary information. For contractual matters — particularly involving employer-employee disputes — provisions of the Indian Contract Act and the Specific Relief Act are key." — [IAM Media](https://www.iam-media.com/article/closer-look-protecting-trade-secrets-in-india-new-legislation-could-be-the-horizon).
- "Causes of action that are commonly asserted against misappropriation and unauthorised disclosure of trade secrets are: **breach of confidence; breach of contract; tort of misappropriation; and criminal offences, such as theft and criminal breach of trust.**" — [IAM Media](https://www.iam-media.com/article/closer-look-protecting-trade-secrets-in-india-new-legislation-could-be-the-horizon).

**Section 27, Indian Contract Act 1872.**
- Section 27: **"Every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind is to that extent void."** — [Maheshwari & Co, "Non-Compete Clauses India: Is It Enforceable?"](https://www.maheshwariandco.com/blog/non-compete-clauses-india-is-it-enforceable/); [Upscale Legal](https://upscalelegal.com/section-27-of-the-indian-contract-act-a-trade-restriction/). India has only **one statutory exception** (sale of goodwill); India deliberately did **not** adopt the English "reasonableness" test wholesale. **[training knowledge on the goodwill exception — VERIFY against indiacode]**
- "Indian Courts have consistently held that while non-compete clauses **during** the term of employment may be valid, **post-employment restrictions are generally void**." — [Maheshwari & Co](https://www.maheshwariandco.com/blog/non-compete-clauses-india-is-it-enforceable/).

**Case law (all verified only to the level of the search summaries shown).**
- ***Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. The Century Spinning and Manufacturing Co. Ltd.***, Supreme Court, **1967 AIR 1098**: "restraints or negative covenants in contracts may be valid if they are **reasonable**. A restriction operating **while the employee is bound to serve the employer exclusively** is not a restraint of trade within Section 27, because the employee is not being prevented from earning a living, only from serving two masters at once." — [Maheshwari & Co](https://www.maheshwariandco.com/blog/non-compete-clauses-india-is-it-enforceable/); [AMLEGALS](https://amlegals.com/navigating-the-legality-of-non-compete-agreements/). *This is the case employers cite for* during-*employment exclusivity and confidentiality; it is routinely over-cited for post-employment restraints, which it does not support.*
- ***Superintendence Company of India (P) Ltd. v. Krishan Murgai***, Supreme Court, decided **9 May 1980** (Tulzapurkar, Untwalia and Sen JJ); reported **(1981) 2 SCC 246**. Facts: employee terminated 24 Nov 1978 (effective 27 Dec 1978), set up a competing concern "Superintendence and Surveillance Inspectorate of India"; employer sued in Delhi HC on 19 April 1979 for ₹55,000 damages and a permanent injunction to enforce Clause 10. Issues: whether a **post-service** restrictive covenant is void under Section 27, and if valid whether enforceable under **Section 57 of the Specific Relief Act 1963**. Held: "the doctrine of restraint of trade applies **only when a contract of employment ends**, and negative covenants **during** the employment period are generally not regarded as restraint of trade." — [Trace Your Case](https://traceyourcase.com/superintendence-co-of-india-ltd-v-krishan-murgai-1981-2-scc-246/); [CaseMine commentary](https://www.casemine.com/commentary/in/enforceability-of-post-service-restraints-in-employment-contracts:-insights-from-superintendence-co.-of-india-v.-murgai/view); [indiankanoon 1186410](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1186410/) (blocked). *The appeal was dismissed; the post-service restraint was not enforced.* **[the disposition is training knowledge — VERIFY]**
- ***Percept D'Mark (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Zaheer Khan***, Supreme Court, **(2006) 4 SCC 227** **[citation is training knowledge — VERIFY]**: "a restrictive covenant **extending beyond the term of the contract is void and not enforceable**. The doctrine of restraint of trade does not apply during the continuance of the contract for employment and is applied only when the contract comes to an end." — [AMLEGALS](https://amlegals.com/navigating-the-legality-of-non-compete-agreements/); [Maheshwari & Co](https://www.maheshwariandco.com/blog/non-compete-clauses-india-is-it-enforceable/). *This is the modern, controlling statement and it is the one to quote.*
- ***American Express Bank Ltd. v. Ms. Priya Puri***, **Delhi High Court, 24 May 2006**. Plaintiff bank sought permanent and mandatory injunctions against its former head of Wealth Management (North), alleging misuse of confidential information, trade secrets and customer data relating to its Wealth Management operations and "iWealth View" programme. **Held: the Delhi High Court dismissed the application for a perpetual injunction and vacated the interim order** that had restrained her from accessing and using confidential customer data, "reinforc[ing] the principle that such restraints are inherently void under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, unless they satisfy stringent exceptions." — [indiankanoon 445135](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/445135/) (blocked); [CaseMine commentary](https://www.casemine.com/commentary/in/enforceability-of-post-employment-restrictive-covenants-under-indian-contract-law%3A-analysis-of-american-express-bank%2C-ltd.-v.-ms.-priya-puri/view); [WIPO Lex entry](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/judgments/details/3372) (blocked). **Key additional holding [training knowledge — VERIFY]: the court took a narrow view of what counts as a trade secret, treating customer lists compiled from publicly available sources and general know-how/skill acquired in employment as *not* protectable, and observed that an employee's right to livelihood under Article 19(1)(g) weighs against broad restraints.**
- ***John Richard Brady v. Chemical Process Equipments P. Ltd.***, **AIR 1987 Delhi 372** **[citation and holding are training knowledge — could NOT be verified in this session]**: Delhi HC granted an injunction protecting technical drawings and know-how disclosed under a supply negotiation, holding the obligation of confidence arose **independently of contract**, on the equitable principle that information imparted in confidence must not be turned to the recipient's own advantage. *This is the foundational Indian authority for the springboard/breach-of-confidence doctrine and for the proposition that a manufacturer to whom you send drawings owes you a duty even without an NDA.* **It is directly on point for the overseas PCB assembly house scenario (Q7) and should be verified before being relied on in a contract negotiation or pleading.**

Naveen Jain***, **122 (2005) DLT 421**, Delhi High Court **[citation and holding are training knowledge — could NOT be verified in this session]**: held that **routine day-to-day affairs of an employer that are known to employees are not trade secrets**, and declined to enforce a post-termination restraint. *This is the case that defeats an over-broad "everything is confidential" clause: if you designate everything as secret, you protect nothing.*

### Inferences
- **The operative asymmetry for the founder, stated plainly:** you can stop a departing employee from *using or disclosing your secret*; you cannot stop them from *going to work for a competitor*. Drafting therefore has to do all its work through (a) a tightly-scoped, specifically-enumerated confidentiality clause, (b) a non-solicitation clause (weaker but sometimes enforced), and (c) *during*-employment exclusivity (safe under *Golikari*). A post-employment non-compete is worth including only as a deterrent for people who will not take legal advice; it will not be enforced and, if litigated, invites the court to look sceptically at the whole agreement.
- ***Priya Puri* is the cautionary tale.** A large bank with a signed contract lost its interim injunction because the court was not persuaded the information was genuinely secret as opposed to general skill and publicly-derivable data. The lesson is evidentiary: **the enforceability of your secret depends on your ability to prove you treated it as one.** Access logs, an inventory of designated secrets, encryption, need-to-know distribution and signed acknowledgements are not compliance theatre — they are the case.
- **Damages are close to illusory in practice for a one-person company.** Indian courts award damages in trade secret cases sparingly and quantification is hard; the realistic goal is an injunction plus delivery-up. Budget for the injunction being the whole of the recovery.
- **Criminal remedies (theft, criminal breach of trust under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, which replaced the IPC in 2024) are leverage rather than a remedy.** Filing an FIR against a former employee who copied a repo is a real option and is often the thing that produces a settlement, but it is aggressive and Indian police are generally reluctant in commercial disputes. **[training knowledge — VERIFY the BNS section numbers before use]**

## Q3. What practically protects a secret inside a shipped hardware product?

### Takeaway
**Law protects you against people who are in a relationship with you; it does not protect you against a competitor who buys your product on the open market and opens it.** Reverse engineering of a lawfully-acquired product is not a wrong in India — there is no statutory prohibition and no confidential relationship to breach. Therefore, for anything that ships inside the device, **technical measures are the protection and the contract is irrelevant**. The technical measures are real but finite: firmware readout protection and flash encryption raise the cost of extraction from "trivial" to "a funded lab with fault-injection equipment," and no more than that. **Design the business on the assumption that a determined competitor will eventually extract everything you ship.**

### Cited Findings
- **STM32 RDP Level 2 is strong but not unbreakable.** RDP2 is irreversible once set, but published research shows bypass vectors: "fault injection attacks like **clock glitching** can reset the MCU during secure boot, or **laser fault injection** can bypass password checks"; one attack "uses fine-grain CPU resets and a vulnerability in the Flash protection logic protocol to extract firmware by accessing iterative addresses via the debugger and capturing latent Flash data in unprotected SRAM." — [HackMag, "Protecting microcontrollers: Implementing Firmware Hardening and Secure Boot on STM32"](https://hackmag.com/security/protec-stm32); [Hackaday, "Breaking Into A Secure Facility: STM32 Flash"](https://hackaday.com/2020/03/24/breaking-into-a-secure-facility-stm32-flash/).
- **ESP32 flash encryption and secure boot have been broken, unpatchably, on early silicon.** "**Voltage glitching** has been used to modify the Read Protection Values of the E-Fuses Controller, achieving a full readout of both the **Flash Encryption Key (FEK) and Secure Boot Key (SBK)**. This attack **cannot be patched by the vendor on existing devices**, making it a permanent compromise." — [LimitedResults via Adafruit](https://blog.adafruit.com/2019/11/18/esp32-flash-encryption-and-sec-boot-keys-extraction-esp32-encryption-pwn-limitedresults/).
- A separate published attack "bypasses ESP32's Secure Boot and Flash Encryption features using a **single EM (electromagnetic) glitch**", applicable to **ESP32 silicon revision 0 and 1**. — [Raelize, "Espressif ESP32: Bypassing Encrypted Secure Boot (CVE-2020-13629)"](https://raelize.com/blog/espressif-esp32-bypassing-encrypted-secure-boot-cve-2020-13629/); [Raelize, "Bypassing Flash Encryption (CVE-2020-15048)"](https://raelize.com/blog/espressif-systems-esp32-bypassing-flash-encryption/).
- Broader academic work confirms MCU IP-protection features are routinely defeated: [RIPencapsulation: Defeating IP Encapsulation on TI MSP Devices](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.16433); ["One Exploit to Rule them All? On the Security of Drop-in Replacement and Counterfeit Microcontrollers"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09710).
- India's current framework "largely leans on **contracts, licensing arrangements and tech safeguards**" for data assets. — [The Data Economy Lab / search synthesis](https://thedataeconomylab.com/2021/06/14/understanding-intellectual-property-rights-issues-around-non-personal-databases/).

### Inferences (engineering/strategy analysis, not cited law)
- **Concrete measures, ranked by cost-effectiveness for a one-person company:**
  1. **Set readout protection and permanently disable the debug port on production units.** STM32 RDP Level 2 (irreversible — note this destroys field debuggability and returns-analysis; you need a separate, clearly-marked engineering build). On ESP32, enable **flash encryption in Release mode** plus **Secure Boot V2**, and **burn the eFuses that disable UART download mode and JTAG**. Use a **current silicon revision** (rev 3+) — the published glitch attacks target rev 0/1.
  2. **Do not ship the model in plaintext.** Store tuned curves, per-scene parameters and learned coefficients in a binary blob encrypted at rest and decrypted into RAM at boot, keyed from a per-device secret in eFuse/OTP. This converts "dump flash and read the JSON" into "dump flash, extract the key, and reverse the format" — a different class of adversary.
  3. **Separate the *shipped default* from the *accumulated learning*.** The factory defaults must ship; the per-home learned model is generated on the customer's device. Even a total firmware extraction yields your starting point, not your fleet-level learning. Only fleet-level aggregate learning (if you ever build it) is genuinely defensible, and only if it stays on your server.
  4. **Obfuscation and constant-splitting** in the firmware: worth a day of effort, not a week. It delays, it does not prevent.
  5. **Unique per-device keys**, so extracting one unit does not compromise the fleet.
- **The server-side option and the offline tension.** Keeping the solver server-side is the only architecture that genuinely protects the algorithm, because the algorithm never leaves your infrastructure. **This product is deliberately offline, so that option is foreclosed for the control loop.** But it is *not* foreclosed for everything: a defensible hybrid is (a) the real-time solver runs on-device (must, for offline operation and latency), while (b) the **calibration/commissioning step** — which happens once, at install, typically with an installer's phone present — could optionally upload the raw sweep measurements and receive back a fitted transport matrix. That keeps the *fitting* method secret while preserving offline operation thereafter. It costs you a hard dependency at install time and a privacy/DPDP surface (see the sibling `dpdp_and_claims.md` note), and it breaks the "works with no internet" promise at the one moment the customer is forming their impression of the product. **My assessment: not worth it for a first product. Accept that the solver ships.**
- **Reverse engineering is lawful.** India has no anti-reverse-engineering statute for hardware. Section 52(1)(ab)–(ad) of the Copyright Act expressly permits certain reverse engineering of computer programmes for interoperability and for obtaining information not otherwise available. **[training knowledge — VERIFY the sub-clause lettering]** There is no contract with a purchaser that would bind them; a shrink-wrap "no reverse engineering" term on a consumer lighting product is of doubtful enforceability and is unlikely ever to be litigated by a company this size.
- **Therefore the honest rule for this product: anything that must execute on the customer's device is, in the long run, public. Anything that can be kept off the device is genuinely secret.** That single distinction drives most of the Q4 ranking.

## Q4. The central trade-off analysis, applied to the six assets, with a ranked recommendation

### Takeaway
**Patent two assets, keep three secret, and abandon one.** The decisive variable is not "how clever is it" but **detectability in a competitor's product** — a patent you cannot detect infringement of is a ₹2–5 lakh receipt for nothing. The calibration sweep is the only algorithmic asset that a competitor's product visibly performs in front of you; the battery contact geometry is the only mechanical asset that is both visible and novel-looking. Everything else fails the detectability test, the patentability test, or both.

### The framework (applied identically to each asset)
For each asset, five questions:
- **(a) Detectability** — can you tell, from outside a rival's product, that it infringes? *If no, a patent is nearly worthless: you can never enforce it.* This is the dominant term.
- **(b) Reverse-engineerability** — will it fall out of a teardown? *If yes, secrecy fails and a patent is the better trade.*
- **(c) Independent discoverability** — will a competent competitor arrive at this on their own? *Trade secrets give NO protection against independent invention; patents do.*
- **(d) Value of the 20-year clock vs indefinite secrecy** — is this still worth anything in 2046? In a category where the hardware platform turns over every 4–6 years, mostly no.
- **(e) Teaching cost of publication** — patenting forces publication at 18 months (Section 11A, Patents Act). Does the published specification hand a competitor a roadmap worth more than the claim blocks?

---

### RANKED RECOMMENDATIONS

#### 1. **PATENT — Asset 1: the calibration sweep producing a per-room light transport matrix, with constrained least-squares to targets and measured daylight subtracted.**
- **(a) Detectability: HIGH — this is the whole reason to patent it.** A rival's product performs the sweep *in front of the installer*: the lights visibly flash one at a time at commissioning. You can buy the competitor's product, install it, film the sweep, and you have your infringement evidence before you have finished the setup wizard. This is as good as detectability gets for a software-implemented method.
- **(b) Reverse-engineerability: HIGH.** The behaviour is observable even without a teardown; the implementation falls out of firmware extraction.
- **(c) Independent discoverability: MODERATE–HIGH.** Light transport matrices are standard in graphics and in professional lighting design; per-fixture sweep calibration is a known technique in theatrical and architectural lighting commissioning. A competent competitor with a lighting background could get here. **This is exactly the case where secrecy gives you nothing and a patent gives you something.**
- **(d) 20-year clock:** the *method* will outlive several hardware generations. Good fit.
- **(e) Teaching cost: LOW relative to benefit.** Because (b) and (c) are both high, the competitor learns it anyway. Publication costs you little that you were going to keep.
- **Cross-reference:** the sibling note `patents_section_3k.md` independently concludes this is "the strongest single feature in the disclosure for 3(k) purposes" and should be "the centre of gravity of the independent claims," because the claim runs physical actuation → physical measurement → physical actuation and so escapes the mathematical-method exclusion. The solver claimed alone would be refused. **The Q4 analysis and the patentability analysis agree: this is the one to file.**
- **Verdict: PATENT. This is the priority filing.** Claim it as a *method of commissioning and operating a lighting system*, with the sweep, the measurement and the driving of luminaires in the independent claim. Do not claim the solver.

#### 2. **PATENT (conditionally) — Asset 4: concentric ring battery contacts, rotationally symmetric, pack dead until mated.**
- **(a) Detectability: VERY HIGH.** It is a visible physical feature. You open the rival's product and photograph it. Best detectability of the six.
- **(b) Reverse-engineerability: TOTAL AND IMMEDIATE.** One teardown. Secrecy is not even theoretically available — you are shipping the thing itself.
- **(c) Independent discoverability: HIGH.** Concentric/coaxial contacts for rotationally-symmetric mating are old (think barrel jacks, slip rings, Apple MagSafe-style designs, cordless-kettle bases). **This is the risk: it may not be novel.** The "pack dead until mated" element — the cell being electrically isolated until a mating-detect condition is satisfied — is the more plausible inventive hook, and it is also a *safety* feature, which reads well to an examiner and has a clear technical effect.
- **(d) 20-year clock:** fine, but the mechanical design will change with the next enclosure.
- **(e) Teaching cost: ZERO.** You are shipping it; publication teaches nothing that a teardown does not.
- **Important alternative:** this is also a candidate for **design registration** under the Designs Act 2000 for the *appearance* (see sibling note `designs_disclosure_trademarks.md`), which is far cheaper and faster than a patent — but a design registration protects only the visual form, not the dead-until-mated function. **A competitor can copy the function with a different-looking contact.**
- **Verdict: PATENT, conditional on a prior art search coming back clean on "dead until mated."** If the search shows the isolation-until-mating idea is anticipated, downgrade to **design registration only** for the ring appearance, and do not spend on a patent. **The prior art search is the gating spend here, not the filing.**

#### 3. **SECRET — Asset 6 (the founder's real crown jewels): the tuned curves, per-scene parameter values, and accumulated behavioural models.**
- **(a) Detectability: NEAR ZERO.** If a rival's "Relax" scene lands at a similar CCT and lux, you cannot prove they took your numbers rather than arriving at plausible values themselves. There is no infringement you could plead, let alone prove.
- **(b) Reverse-engineerability: HIGH but expensive, and *incomplete*.** Extracting the shipped defaults is a firmware dump. Extracting the *accumulated* learning is not possible from a device you do not own, because it lives on individual customers' units.
- **(c) Independent discoverability: HIGH for the curves** (a competitor with a photometer, a room and three weeks of taste will produce curves that are good enough), **LOW for the accumulated fleet behaviour** (they would need your installed base and your time).
- **(d) Clock:** indefinite secrecy strictly dominates — this asset *appreciates* with every install, which is the exact opposite of a 20-year decaying monopoly.
- **(e) Teaching cost: CATASTROPHIC.** A published table of your tuned values is a gift: it hands a competitor the output of all your tuning work in a form they can transcribe, while the patent claim (if you even got one) would be unenforceable per (a).
- **Patentable at all? Almost certainly not.** See Q6 — these are data and parameter values, not an invention; they are not novel in any patent sense and there is no technical teaching. An attempt would be refused and would have published the numbers.
- **Verdict: SECRET. And the founder's instinct is correct — see Q6 for the full analysis.**

#### 4. **SECRET — Asset 3: learning from user overrides only, against a context vector including measured outdoor light.**
- **(a) Detectability: LOW.** You could, with effort, black-box a rival unit — override it repeatedly under varied outdoor conditions and see whether it adapts and on what axes. That is real but it is weeks of work per accusation, it is contestable, and it does not distinguish "learns from overrides only" from "learns from overrides plus dwell time." **Marginal, expensive, disputable detectability = a patent you will not enforce.**
- **(b) Reverse-engineerability: HIGH.** Falls out of firmware.
- **(c) Independent discoverability: VERY HIGH.** "Treat the user's correction as the training signal, conditioned on context" is the obvious design. Nest did implicit-feedback learning on thermostats over a decade ago. **This is the asset most likely to be independently invented, which argues for patenting — except that (a) kills it.**
- **(d)/(e):** publication would teach the architecture cheaply and buy an unenforceable claim.
- **Honest caveat:** the *specific negative limitation* — learning **only** from overrides, and deliberately **not** from other signals — is the kind of counter-intuitive restriction that sometimes makes a decent claim. But a negative limitation is exactly what you cannot detect in someone else's product.
- **Verdict: SECRET.** Consider **defensive publication** instead if the founder is worried about a competitor patenting it and blocking *him* — see Q5. That worry is the only reason to disclose.

#### 5. **NEITHER (secret by default; do not spend) — Asset 2: target-state control (user picks Read/Relax/Cook, the system solves for the room state).**
- **(a) Detectability: TOTAL** (it is the UI).
- **(b) Reverse-engineerability: TOTAL** (it is the UI).
- **(c) Independent discoverability: CERTAIN. This is almost certainly not novel.** Scene presets that name an activity and drive a lighting state are ubiquitous — Philips Hue, Lutron, LIFX, Casambi, DALI scenes, and every commercial lighting controller since the 1990s. The *solving* part is Asset 1.
- **Patentability: very poor.** Stripped to what is new over the prior art, this is a preset button. Under the Indian test (see `patents_section_3k.md`), if the only contribution is the abstract goal-state-and-solve step, Section 3(k) bites; and independently it fails novelty.
- **Verdict: NEITHER.** Do not patent (it will be refused and it will cost you the fee and the drafting). Do not treat it as a secret (it is the product's front page). **Protect the *name* and the *look* instead** — the scene names and iconography are trademark and design-registration territory, which is cheap. Cross-reference `designs_disclosure_trademarks.md`.

#### 6. **NEITHER / DESIGN REGISTRATION, NOT PATENT — Asset 5: the three-layer circular module physical design.**
- **(a) Detectability: TOTAL.**
- **(b) Reverse-engineerability: TOTAL.**
- **(c) Independent discoverability: HIGH.** A three-layer stack in a circular puck is what every circular IoT device looks like, because it is what fits.
- **Patentability: poor.** A physical arrangement of known layers is squarely at risk under **Section 3(f)** — "mere arrangement or re-arrangement of known devices each functioning independently." The sibling `patents_section_3k.md` note independently flags 3(f) as the second-biggest exclusion risk for this product. Unless a specific layer interaction produces a non-obvious technical effect (thermal path, antenna keep-out, sensor isolation from the LED's own light — **that last one might be real**), there is no invention here.
- **Verdict: NEITHER as a patent. File a DESIGN REGISTRATION** under the Designs Act 2000 for the appearance — it is cheap, fast, and it is the right instrument for "the product looks like this." **Caveat: design registration requires novelty and is destroyed by prior publication, so file before any public disclosure, photos, crowdfunding page or trade show.** See `designs_disclosure_trademarks.md`.
- **One exception worth a second look:** if the three-layer stack is what allows the ambient/daylight sensor to measure the room without being swamped by the module's own LEDs, **that is a technical effect and it supports a system claim** — and it should be folded into the Asset 1 patent as a dependent claim rather than filed separately.

## Q5. Defensive publication as a third option

### Takeaway
Defensive publication is deliberate public disclosure to create **prior art**, so that **nobody — including you — can later patent the idea**. It costs a tiny fraction of a patent and requires no prosecution. **It beats both patenting and secrecy in exactly one situation: where you do not need to exclude anyone, but you are afraid that a competitor will patent the idea and exclude *you*.** For this founder, Asset 3 (learning from overrides) is the clearest candidate.

### Cited Findings
- "A defensive publication is a deliberate public disclosure made to create prior art that blocks a patent — **by anyone, including the discloser** — without filing one." — [Wikipedia, Defensive publication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defensive_publication).
- Venues: "commercial services such as **IP.com's Prior Art Database**, the journal **Research Disclosure**, or **Proofbox**, as well as through the free repository **Technical Disclosure Commons**." — search synthesis over [IP.com Defensive Publishing](https://ip.com/innovation-power-suite/defensive-publishing/) and [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defensive_publication).
- Cost: "**Defensive publishing blocks competitor patents for ~2% of filing costs.**" "**IP.com requires publishers to pay a fee, while Technical Disclosure Commons is a free repository.**" — search synthesis over [Arctic Invent](https://www.arcticinvent.com/blog/defensive-publications-to-gain-a-competitive-edge) and [IP.com KB](https://kb.ip.com/pad/knowledge-base/introduction-to-defensive-publishing-and-the-prior-art-database-pad/).
- Examiner reach: "Under **Rule 34 of the Patent Cooperation Treaty**, the [IP.com] Prior Art Database is a required reference for international patent examiners." — [IP.com KB](https://kb.ip.com/pad/knowledge-base/introduction-to-defensive-publishing-and-the-prior-art-database-pad/). *This is the key quality difference between IP.com and a blog post: PCT ISAs are obliged to search it.*
- Practitioner guidance on doing it properly: [Perkins Coie, "When and How to Publish Technical Disclosures as Prior Art"](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/publication/when-and-how-publish-technical-disclosures-as-prior-art).

### Inferences
- **How to do it so it actually creates citable prior art.** Prior art must be (i) **publicly accessible**, (ii) **dated beyond argument**, and (iii) **enabling** — i.e. it must teach a skilled person enough to practise the idea. A vague marketing blog post fails (iii) and often (ii). Practical recipe, best to worst:
  1. **IP.com Prior Art Database** — paid, but PCT Rule 34 minimum documentation, so examiners are *required* to search it. Best quality of art. Use for anything you genuinely fear being patented against you.
  2. **Technical Disclosure Commons** (free, run by Google/Santa Clara Law) — indexed, DOI-like permanent URLs, credible dates. Excellent value at zero cost.
  3. **arXiv** — free, immutable dated versions, widely indexed. Good if the disclosure can be framed as a technical write-up. Category fit for a lighting control method is awkward (eess.SY or cs.SY would work).
  4. **A dated blog post** — weakest. Works only if archived independently (Internet Archive / Wayback snapshot on the day of publication) and written in enabling technical detail. Use as a supplement, never alone.
  - **Write it like a patent specification, not like marketing:** state the problem, the full method with parameters and ranges, at least one worked embodiment, and deliberately broad alternatives ("in some embodiments the context vector further comprises…"). Breadth matters — art only blocks what it discloses.
- **The downsides, stated honestly:** (1) it is **irrevocable** — you have destroyed your own novelty worldwide, and India has only a narrow 12-month grace period under Section 31 for specified circumstances (exhibitions, learned-society papers), which a defensive publication does **not** fit **[training knowledge — VERIFY Section 31]**; (2) it **teaches your competitor**, immediately and for free, whereas a patent at least takes 18 months to publish; (3) it gives you **no right to exclude anyone** — it is purely defensive; (4) it can make your own later, better invention harder to patent if it is an obvious increment on what you published.
- **Decision rule for this founder:** publish defensively only where *all* of: you will not patent it, you could not keep it secret anyway (it ships and is observable), and a competitor patenting it would genuinely block you. **Asset 3 fits.** Asset 2 (scene presets) arguably fits too but the prior art is already so thick that nobody will get a patent on it — do nothing. **Never defensively publish Asset 6 (tuned curves).** That would be handing over the crown jewels to prevent a patent that nobody could obtain in the first place.
- **Sequencing matters:** any defensive publication must come **after** the Asset 1 provisional is filed, and must be scrubbed so that it does not disclose the calibration-sweep claim. Cross-reference `designs_disclosure_trademarks.md` on disclosure destroying design novelty too.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain a current price for IP.com Prior Art Database publication (historically a few hundred USD per disclosure, but unverified).
- I did not verify whether the Indian Patent Office's examiners in practice search IP.com / Technical Disclosure Commons. PCT Rule 34 binds ISAs; whether an IPO examiner doing a national search does so is unknown.

## Q6. Is the founder's instinct right that learned data and tuned curves are worth more unpublished than patented?

### Takeaway
**Yes — and more strongly than the founder probably realises, because there are three independent reasons and any one of them would be sufficient.** (1) **Not patentable subject matter**: parameter values are data, not an invention. (2) **Not detectable**: even with a granted patent you could never prove infringement. (3) **Not protectable as a database**: India has **no sui generis database right**, and copyright in a compilation requires a "modicum of creativity" per *Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak*, which a table of measured/tuned numbers will struggle to meet. Taken together: the only thing standing between these assets and a competitor is **secrecy plus contract plus encryption**. The founder is right; the correct response is to build the secrecy programme properly, not to look for a registration.

### Cited Findings

**No sui generis database right.**
- "**India does not have a sui generis database right**, so it largely leans on contracts, licensing arrangements and tech safeguards." — [The Data Economy Lab, "Understanding IPR issues around Non-Personal Databases"](https://thedataeconomylab.com/2021/06/14/understanding-intellectual-property-rights-issues-around-non-personal-databases/) (search summary). Contrast: "the **EU Database Directive** contains a sui generis right granting protection to substantial investment in obtaining, verifying, or presenting database contents." Corroborated by [Law School Policy Review, "Sui Generis Database Right: A Long Last Road for India"](https://lawschoolpolicyreview.com/2019/12/22/sui-generis-database-right-a-long-last-road-for-india/) — the title of which is itself evidence that India has not adopted one.

**Copyright in a database requires originality — "modicum of creativity."**
- ***Eastern Book Company & Ors. v. D.B. Modak & Anr.*** (Supreme Court of India, 2008): "explained that the threshold for originality is a **'modicum of creativity'** and it **abandoned the 'sweat of the brow' doctrine**, which protected mere labour and effort." — [Intepat, "Originality In Database: Understanding Copyright Thresholds"](https://www.intepat.com/blog/originality-in-database-understanding-copyright-thresholds); [WIPO Lex entry](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/judgments/details/2840) (blocked); [IJLLR, "Originality After Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak"](https://www.ijllr.com/post/originality-after-eastern-book-company-v-d-b-modak-has-india-found-its-own-copyright-threshold).
- Facts and outcome: EBC published law reports containing "editorial inputs like headnotes, summaries, and formatting, which were later reproduced by D.B. Modak without consent"; **the editorial inputs were found to reflect sufficient creativity and qualified for copyright**, while the raw judgment text did not. — [Luke & Luka, "Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak — Copyright in Compilations (2008)"](https://lukeandluka.in/insights/eastern-book-company-v-db-modak-copyright-judicial-decisions-2008/); [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/originality-in-database-understanding-copyright-thresholds).
- "In determining originality, the courts in India have **shifted from the 'sweat of the brow' test to the 'modicum of creativity' approach**, where the Supreme Court held that some amount of creativity in the work is required to claim a copyright." — [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/originality-in-database-understanding-copyright-thresholds).

**Patentability.**
- Under Section 3(k) the exclusion covers "a mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms," aimed at preventing "the patenting of abstract ideas, algorithms, and business methods without technical application"; the 2025 CRI Guidelines apply a step-wise test in which, "if novelty lies only in abstract steps or code," a 3(k) objection follows. — sibling note `patents_section_3k.md`, citing [Intepat, CRI Guidelines 2025](https://www.intepat.com/blog/cri-guidelines-2025-patent-india-3k) and [Lexology, Analyzing the Draft CRI Guidelines 2025 – Part 5](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b35781d8-4ffe-4a05-9257-ff752fc40cdb).

### Inferences
- **A tuned curve is not an invention.** A patent claim must define an invention — a product or a process. "A colour temperature of 2700K at 30% output for a 'Relax' scene" is a *value*, not a process. There is no inventive step in a number; there is no technical teaching; and even if you dressed it as "a lighting system wherein the Relax scene comprises CCT in the range 2400–2900K," the claim would be anticipated by any warm-white scene preset ever shipped and would be obvious besides. **Filing would be refused and would publish your numbers.** That is the worst of every world.
- **Accumulated behavioural models: what protection is actually available?** Three layers, all weak-to-moderate, and they must be stacked:
  1. **Copyright** — only in the *structure and selection* of the database, and only if it clears *EBC v. Modak*'s modicum of creativity. A schema you designed, with a non-obvious selection of context features and a considered organisation, has a reasonable claim. **The raw numbers do not.** And the practical utility is limited: a competitor who derives equivalent numbers from their own users infringes nothing.
  2. **Confidence / trade secret** — the real protection, and the only one that reaches the *content*. Requires you to have taken reasonable steps to keep it secret (encryption, access control, NDAs, designation).
  3. **Contract** — binds employees, contractors and the CM. Does not bind a purchaser who reverse-engineers.
- **Note the DPDP interaction.** Behavioural data from identifiable homes is personal data under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, which cuts *against* hoarding it centrally: you acquire compliance obligations that reduce the asset's net value. Cross-reference the sibling `dpdp_and_claims.md` note. **A strong argument for the offline/on-device architecture is that it sidesteps this entirely — at the cost of you never accumulating a fleet-level dataset.** The founder should decide consciously whether the fleet dataset is a strategic asset he wants; if it is, he must design consent and minimisation in from the start. If it is not, on-device-only learning is both the better privacy story and the better secrecy story, because there is no central honeypot to breach.
- **Where the value actually sits, ranked:** (i) the **fleet-level aggregate learning** you never ship — genuinely defensible, indefinitely, and appreciating; (ii) the **tuned default curves** — defensible until the first competent teardown, which for a small product might be years; (iii) the **per-home learned model** — not an asset at all in IP terms, it is a feature of the customer's unit.
- **Therefore: the founder's instinct is right, but the corollary is a duty, not a relief.** "Keep it secret" is not a decision you make once; it is an operating discipline. See Q7 and the programme notes below.

### Gaps
- I could not verify whether any Indian court has ruled on copyright in a **machine-learning model's weights** or in a **parameter set**. To my knowledge none has; this is genuinely unsettled everywhere. Flag as an open question rather than asserting a position.
- Whether India has moved on database rights since 2024 (there were DPIIT/MeitY consultations on non-personal data governance) — not researched, low likelihood of change.
- The exact SCC citation for *EBC v. Modak* is (2008) 1 SCC 1 **[training knowledge — VERIFY]**.

## Q7. Employee and contractor IP: what must be in place

### Takeaway
**The single most important asymmetry in Indian law for this founder: copyright in works made in the course of employment vests in the employer automatically under Section 17 of the Copyright Act 1957, but PATENT rights do NOT — there is no equivalent provision in the Patents Act 1970, so patent ownership requires EXPRESS ASSIGNMENT.** A company that hires an engineer, gets the firmware (copyright, fine) but does *not* get the inventions unless the contract says so. For contractors and freelancers the position is worse: **Section 17 does not help you at all for a contract *for* services**, so a freelance industrial designer owns the copyright in their drawings by default. And there is no work-for-hire doctrine in India to rescue you.

### Cited Findings

**Copyright — automatic, for employees.**
- "**Section 17 of the Indian Copyright Act, 1957 specifically assigns the copyright to the employer if made in the course of employment (unless there is a contract to the contrary).** More specifically, if a work is created by an author in the course of employment **under a contract of service or apprenticeship**, the employer shall be considered the first owner of the copyright in the absence of any contrary agreement." — [SpicyIP, "The Dilemma of Employer-Employee Patent Ownership in India"](https://spicyip.com/2021/05/the-dilemma-of-employer-employee-patent-ownership-in-india.html) (search summary); [Khaitan & Co, "Intellectual property clause (employment) Q&A: India"](https://www.khaitanco.com/sites/default/files/2021-07/Intellectual%20property%20clause%20(employment)%20QAndA%20India.pdf).
- The relevant sub-clause is **Section 17(c)** (contract of service or apprenticeship); Section 17(b) deals with commissioned photographs, paintings, portraits and cinematograph films. **[sub-clause lettering is training knowledge — VERIFY against indiacode]**

**Patents — express assignment required.**
- "Employees assign their patent rights to the employer through **pre-assignment** (usually part of the employment contract) and **assignment agreements** (signed after the invention is realised). This express assignment is necessary because the **Indian Patents Act, 1970 has a lacuna — no provision in the Act clarifies the ownership of the patent invented by the employee** under the aegis of the employer." — [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2021/05/the-dilemma-of-employer-employee-patent-ownership-in-india.html); corroborated by [Khurana & Khurana](https://www.khuranaandkhurana.com/copyright-ownership-in-employment-and-freelance-contracts-key-legal-insights) and [Mondaq, "Who Owns What? The IP Rights That Arise From Employee-Created AI Workflows"](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/1810146/who-owns-what-the-ip-rights-that-arise-from-employee-created-artificial-intelligence-workflows).
- "**While the Copyright Act has Section 17 which specifically assigns copyright to the employer if made in the course of employment, the Patents Act does not contain a similar provision**, despite being a newer statute. This creates a significant practical difference: **copyright automatically vests in the employer upon creation during employment, whereas patent ownership requires explicit contractual assignment** to transfer ownership from the employee (the statutory 'first owner') to the employer." — [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2021/05/the-dilemma-of-employer-employee-patent-ownership-in-india.html).

**Who may apply — Section 6, Patents Act 1970.**
- Section 6 provides that an application may be made by **(a) any person claiming to be the true and first inventor; (b) any person being the assignee of the person claiming to be the true and first inventor; or (c) the legal representative of any deceased person who immediately before his death was entitled to make such an application.** **[training knowledge — VERIFY against indiacode; this is standard and low-risk but should be checked.]** The practical consequence: **a company is never the "true and first inventor" — it can only apply as an assignee**, which is precisely why the assignment must exist.

**Non-competes.** See Q2 — post-employment non-competes are void under Section 27; confidentiality obligations are enforceable.

### Inferences — what must actually be in the contracts

**A) Employment agreement (for the first hire and every hire after).** Non-negotiable clauses:
1. **Present assignment of future inventions**, worded in the present tense: *"Employee hereby assigns to the Company all right, title and interest in all Inventions…"* — not "agrees to assign," which is merely a promise and leaves you suing for specific performance. (US courts have drawn exactly this distinction; Indian courts have not squarely ruled, so use the safer wording.)
2. **Further-assurances clause**: an obligation to sign any deed, declaration or Form the Company reasonably requires, including after termination, plus an **irrevocable power of attorney** to execute IP documents if the employee will not. *This is the clause that saves you when an ex-employee is uncooperative at the moment you need a proof-of-right document (Q8).*
3. **Confidentiality clause**, surviving termination indefinitely, with **specifically enumerated categories** of confidential information (name the tuned curves, the calibration methodology, the firmware source, the BOM and supplier pricing). *Per* **Ambiance India**, *a clause that claims everything protects nothing; per* **Priya Puri**, *the court will ask whether the information is genuinely secret.*
4. **Non-solicitation** of employees and customers for a defined, short period. Weaker than confidentiality, sometimes enforced. Worth including.
5. **During-employment exclusivity / no moonlighting on competing products.** Safe under **Golikari**.
6. **Return and deletion of materials** on exit, with a signed exit certificate confirming it. *This is an evidentiary document you will be glad to have.*
7. **Do NOT rely on a post-employment non-compete.** Include it if you like as a deterrent, but never let it be the load-bearing clause, and be aware that an aggressive one may make a court less sympathetic to the whole agreement.

**B) Freelance industrial designer (contract FOR services).**
- **Section 17(c) does not apply.** Absent an express written assignment, **the designer owns the copyright in their drawings, CAD files and renders**, and you would have at best an implied licence for the purpose commissioned. That implied licence probably does not extend to giving the files to a different manufacturer, to a redesign, or to a successor product.
- Therefore the contract must contain: (i) **express written assignment of copyright** — and note that under Section 19 of the Copyright Act an assignment must be **in writing and signed** by the assignor, must specify the work, the rights, the duration and the territory, or it may be defective **[training knowledge — VERIFY Section 19 requirements, including the rule that an unexercised assignment may lapse after five years and that territory defaults to India if unspecified]**; (ii) **assignment of any design rights** and an obligation not to file any design application; (iii) **assignment of inventions** and a further-assurances clause; (iv) **a waiver, so far as permissible, of moral rights** under Section 57 — note Indian moral rights (paternity and integrity) are **not fully waivable**, so the realistic term is a covenant not to assert them; (v) **confidentiality**, surviving; (vi) **a warranty of originality and non-infringement** plus indemnity — you do not want to discover the designer reused a client's shape; (vii) **an express prohibition on portfolio publication without written consent, and in any event not before your design registration is filed** — *this is the clause founders forget, and a designer's Behance post can destroy the novelty of your design registration.*

**C) Overseas PCB assembly house.** The risks here are different and, for a hardware company, larger than the employee risk:
- **The CM has your Gerbers, BOM, firmware binary and test fixtures.** That is the complete product.
- **Risks to address explicitly:** (i) **overbuild / "third shift" production** — the CM running your panel after hours and selling the surplus; (ii) **BOM and design leakage** to their other customers; (iii) **the CM asserting ownership** of process improvements or of tooling; (iv) **firmware image leakage** (the single worst one, because it contains Asset 6).
- **Contract terms that must be there:** written NDA executed **before** any file transfer, with survival; **express statement that all IP in the designs, files, firmware and tooling is and remains yours**, and that any improvements the CM makes in the course of the work are assigned to you; **explicit prohibition on overbuild**, with a requirement to report and destroy or deliver rejects and surplus, and a right to audit; **prohibition on using your files for any other customer**; **serialisation and unit accounting**; **tooling ownership and a right to take possession of tooling on termination**; **no subcontracting without consent**; **governing law and a realistic dispute forum** — and here be honest: **suing a Chinese or Southeast Asian CM from India is slow and expensive and you will probably not do it.** Prefer **Singapore-seated arbitration (SIAC)** over Indian courts if you have any negotiating leverage, because an arbitral award is enforceable across borders under the New York Convention where an Indian decree effectively is not.
- **Because the contract is weak in practice, use structural measures:** (i) **do not send the production firmware to the CM at all** — have boards shipped to you or to a trusted party unprogrammed, and do final programming plus eFuse/RDP lock-down yourself; this is entirely feasible at low volume and it is the single highest-value protective decision available; (ii) **split the supply chain** if volume ever justifies it; (iii) **keep the calibration/test software and the golden reference in your hands**; (iv) **serialise and provision keys yourself.**
- ***John Richard Brady* is the fallback:** Indian equity imposes a duty of confidence on a party to whom technical drawings are disclosed for a limited purpose **even without a contract** **[VERIFY]**. But relying on it against an offshore entity is a theoretical comfort, not a plan.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact text of Section 17 or its sub-clauses, Section 19, or Section 57 of the Copyright Act, nor Section 6 of the Patents Act, from indiacode.nic.in (egress blocked). All of these should be read before drafting.
- Whether Indian courts have enforced a "hereby assigns" present-assignment clause against a reluctant employee-inventor — I found no case. This appears to be genuinely under-litigated in India, which is itself worth telling the founder: **the belt-and-braces approach (contract clause + separate deed at invention time + POA) is warranted precisely because the law is untested.**

## Q8. Inventorship and ownership hygiene: getting the company named as applicant

### Takeaway
**The founder is the inventor; the company should be the applicant; the document that bridges them is an assignment (a deed of assignment, or the inventor's declaration in Form 1), and it must be on file within SIX MONTHS of filing the application** under Section 7(2) of the Patents Act 1970 read with Rule 10. Get this wrong and the application is objected to; get it wrong at scale (multiple inventors, one of whom has left) and it can become unfixable.

### Cited Findings
- "**Section 7(2) of the Act read with Rule 10 of the Patent Rules** states that a **proof of right** to make the application should be furnished **with the application, or within a period of six months of filing the application**." — search synthesis over [Obhan Mason, "Proof of Right Requirement in Patents Law"](https://obhanmason.com/blog/proof-of-right-requirement-in-patents-law/), [Mondaq, "Proof Of Right Requirement In Patents"](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/1087890/proof-of-right-requirement-in-patents), [Selvam & Selvam](https://selvams.com/blog/proof-of-right-for-indian-patent-applications/).
- "Under **Rule 138**, a maximum of **one month extension** can be given for filing Proof of Right if the request for such extension is made **within six months** of the filing of the application." — same sources.
- **Two acceptable forms of proof of right:** "**Form 1** contains a declaration which can be **signed by the inventor(s)** to declare that they are the true and first inventor(s) and the applicant is their assignee or legal representative." Alternatively, "an applicant (assignee) can submit the **original assignment deed** existing between the true and first inventor and the applicant (assignee) to meet the proof of right requirement." — same sources.
- "Non-submission of these documents within the six-month time period from the date of filing the application in India may entail a **late submission fee**." — same sources.
- The Madras High Court has provided clarity on proof of right (a decision reported around 2023–24 taking a purposive view and allowing defects to be cured, rather than treating non-filing as fatal) — [Lexology, "Proof of Right under the Patents Act, 1970 — Madras High Court provides clarity"](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b2a831c7-dc4a-4b62-8597-3139079809d2) (blocked; **title only — I could not read the holding and must not state it**).
- Section 6 (who may apply) — see Q7.

### Inferences — the actual checklist
1. **Name the human being(s) as inventor(s), accurately.** Inventorship in India, as elsewhere, is a question of fact about who conceived the invention. Do not add a co-founder or an investor as an inventor out of courtesy, and do not omit a real contributor. Wrong inventorship is a ground of challenge.
2. **Name the company as applicant.** A company can only apply as **assignee** under Section 6(b) — it cannot be the true and first inventor.
3. **Execute a written deed of assignment from the founder-inventor to the company, dated BEFORE the filing date**, assigning the invention and the right to apply in India and worldwide, and the right to claim priority. Stamp it as required under the relevant state Stamp Act. **[stamping requirement is training knowledge — VERIFY the applicable state's schedule; Karnataka/Maharashtra/Delhi differ]**
4. **File proof of right**: either the inventor's signed declaration in **Form 1** at filing (simplest — the founder signs both as inventor and, for the company, as director), **or** the assignment deed within **six months** (extendable by one month under Rule 138 if requested within the six).
5. **Do it at the provisional stage, not later.** If a provisional is filed in the founder's personal name and the company is incorporated afterwards, you will need a clean assignment from the founder to the company and the chain must be documented. Cleanest is: incorporate first, file in the company's name with a pre-dated assignment.
6. **Future hires:** every employment agreement must carry the present-assignment plus further-assurances plus POA package (Q7), so that a departed engineer cannot hold a Form 1 signature hostage six months into prosecution.
7. **Cross-reference `patents_section_3k.md`** for two adjacent traps that bite at the same moment: (i) **Section 39 foreign filing licence** — as an Indian resident the founder must file in India first and wait six weeks, or obtain a licence on Form 25, before any foreign filing; breach carries imprisonment up to two years and revocation exposure; (ii) the **12-month provisional-to-complete deadline is absolute** with no extension or condonation.
8. **Entity status affects fees:** a private limited company is **not** a "natural person" and only gets the reduced fee slab if it is a **DPIIT-recognised Startup or a registered MSME**, evidenced on **Form 28**. Get the DPIIT recognition before filing — it is free and it roughly halves the official fees. (Per `patents_section_3k.md`.)

### Gaps
- **I could not read the Madras High Court proof-of-right decision** and so cannot state whether a late or defective proof of right is curable. This matters: if it is curable, the six-month deadline is a cost issue; if it is not, it is fatal. **Verify before advising a client to be relaxed about it.**
- Stamp duty rates on an IP assignment deed vary by state and were not researched.
- Whether Form 1 signed by the same natural person in two capacities (inventor and director of the applicant company) is accepted without a separate deed — universally done in practice, but I could not verify it against a Patent Office manual.

## Cross-cutting: running a trade secret programme in a one-person company

### Takeaway
The legal test everywhere — under Indian equity now, and under the draft Bill if it passes — is whether you took **reasonable steps** to keep the information secret. For a one-person company the whole programme can be built in a weekend and maintained in an hour a month. **Its purpose is dual: it deters, and it creates the evidence that wins the interim injunction.**

### Inferences (practical programme; this section is analysis, not cited law)
1. **Write a one-page Confidential Information Register.** List, specifically: the tuned scene curves and parameter files; the calibration/fitting methodology and solver source; the override-learning design and context vector definition; the firmware source tree; the BOM with supplier names and pricing; the test fixture design; customer and installer lists. Date it. Update it when something is added. **This one document does more work in a courtroom than any clause, because it answers the *Priya Puri* question — "what exactly was secret?" — with a document you wrote before the dispute.**
2. **Mark files.** A header comment and a footer on every document: *"Confidential — [Company] — not for disclosure."* Trivial, and it defeats the "I didn't know it was confidential" defence.
3. **Technical access control:** private repository with logged access; secrets and keys in a manager, never in the repo; the tuned-curve files encrypted at rest and never emailed; 2FA everywhere; a per-recipient watermark or unique variant on anything you must send out. Keep the access logs — they are evidence.
4. **NDA before disclosure, every time, no exceptions** — to the CM, the designer, prospective investors (though most VCs refuse; disclose only the non-core then), pilot customers, distributors. Keep the signed copies in one folder.
5. **Compartmentalise.** The CM never needs the firmware source or the curve files. The designer never needs the algorithm. Send the minimum.
6. **On the device:** RDP/eFuse lockdown, encrypted model blob, per-device keys (Q3).
7. **Exit process** even for a single contractor: written confirmation of return and deletion, access revoked the same day, a short exit letter reminding them of the surviving confidentiality obligation. Keep it.
8. **Provenance:** timestamp your development. Signed git commits, or a periodic hash of the source tree posted somewhere dated. If you ever have to prove you had the curves before the competitor, this is how.
9. **Review annually** — and, if the Trade Secrets Bill is enacted, review immediately against the statutory definition and the "reasonable steps" standard it adopts.

### Gaps
- No Indian authority found that enumerates what counts as "reasonable steps" — Indian courts have not developed a checklist the way US courts have under the DTSA/UTSA. The programme above is drawn from general practice, not from cited Indian law, and is labelled as such.
