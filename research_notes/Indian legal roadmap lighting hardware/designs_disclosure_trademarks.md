## (A1) What is registrable as a design under the Designs Act 2000 — Sections 2(d) and 4

### Takeaway
India protects only the eye-appealing, non-functional outward appearance of an article; it must be new or original, undisclosed anywhere in the world before filing, and *significantly distinguishable* from known designs. A bare cylinder will struggle; a cylinder with a specific, articulable set of visual choices (proportions, seam/joint treatment, chamfers, knurling, diffuser geometry, colour-and-shape combination) is the registrable subject matter.

### Cited Findings
- Section 2(d) defines "design" as the features of **shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to any article**, in 2D or 3D or both, by any industrial process or means, which in the finished article **appeal to and are judged solely by the eye** — [ipindia / Designs Act 2000](https://ipindia.gov.in/the-designs-act-2000); [Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas overview](https://www.amsshardul.com/insight/an-overview-of-the-indian-design-system-under-the-designs-act-2000/)
- Section 2(d) expressly **excludes**: any mode or principle of construction; anything which is in substance a mere mechanical device; any trade mark as defined in s.2(1)(v) of the Trade and Merchandise Marks Act 1958 / s.2(1)(zb) of the Trade Marks Act 1999; property marks under s.479 IPC; and any **artistic work** as defined in s.2(c) of the Copyright Act 1957 — [Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas](https://www.amsshardul.com/insight/an-overview-of-the-indian-design-system-under-the-designs-act-2000/); [Mondaq, Understanding the Designs Act 2000](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/1507616/understanding-the-designs-act-2000-safeguarding-creativity-and-innovation)
- Section 4 bars registration where the design: **(a)** is not new or original; **(b)** has been **disclosed to the public anywhere in India or in any other country by publication in tangible form or by use or in any other way prior to the filing date** (or priority date); **(c)** is **not significantly distinguishable from known designs or combination of known designs**; **(d)** comprises or contains scandalous or obscene matter — [ipindia / Designs Act 2000](https://ipindia.gov.in/the-designs-act-2000); [iPleaders, Mode and manner of registration](https://blog.ipleaders.in/mode-and-manner-of-registration-under-designs-act-2000/)
- Practical consequence of s.4(c): "a design is likely to be rejected if it is found to be materially similar to a publicly known design(s)" — [The Legal School, Designs Act 2000](https://thelegalschool.in/blog/design-act-2000)
- Functionality bar in practice: Indian registrable-design analysis turns on whether features are dictated solely by function; purely functional configurations are excluded — [Intepat, Registrable and Non-Registrable Designs in India](https://www.intepat.com/blog/registrable-and-non-registrable-designs-india)

### Inferences
- A **plain, undecorated circular disc / short cylinder is very likely to fail s.4(a) and s.4(c)**: a cylinder is a known geometric form, and a registration claiming "a cylindrical lighting module" without more is materially similar to a large body of known luminaire designs.
- The registrable claim for Modulark is almost certainly **not "a cylinder"** but the **specific visual composition**: the three-layer stacked-disc silhouette, the proportional ratio between wall disc / battery pack / light head, the visible seam or reveal lines where the layers meet, the edge profile (chamfer/fillet radius), the diffuser face treatment, and any surface pattern. Each of these should be described in the statement of novelty.
- The **statement of novelty is the single most important drafting decision**. Claiming novelty in "shape and configuration as illustrated" is weak for a generic form; claiming novelty in the *specific combination* of named features is defensible but narrower. Narrow-and-valid beats broad-and-cancelled (see Crocs, below).
- The three-layer stacking that makes the module *swappable* is a **mode/principle of construction and a mechanical device** — excluded from design protection by s.2(d). Only its *visual expression* is protectable. The mechanism itself is patent territory.

### Gaps
- No Indian case found squarely on the registrability of a simple cylindrical/disc luminaire. The analysis above is reasoning from the general standard, not from a on-point authority.
- Could not verify the exact current text of s.4 from a primary government source — indiacode.nic.in and indiankanoon.org are both blocked by this environment's egress proxy. Text is corroborated across multiple independent secondary sources, but the report-writer should re-verify against the bare act before publication.

## (A2) Case law on design novelty and "significantly distinguishable"

### Takeaway
Four cases set the practical bar: **Reckitt Benckiser v Wyeth** (foreign registration/publication destroys Indian novelty), **Crocs v Bata** (a weak design gets cancelled and you pay the other side's costs), **Diageo v Great Galleon** (infringement judged through the *instructed eye* aware of prior art, not the average consumer), **Whirlpool v Videocon** (a strong, distinctive registration does get enforced, with injunction and damages).

### Cited Findings
- **Reckitt Benckiser (India) Ltd v Wyeth Ltd**, Full Bench, Delhi HC, 15 March 2013: concerned an S-shaped spatula; Wyeth argued prior publication via registrations in the US and UK. The Full Bench clarified that **mere existence of a design in a foreign registrar's records may not amount to prior publication in all cases**, but where the design is registered abroad **and the records are public, it is "published"** and therefore lacks novelty in India — [SpicyIP, Full Bench Delhi HC](https://spicyip.com/2013/03/full-bench-delhi-hc-design-act-reckitt.html); [HG.org summary](https://www.hg.org/legal-articles/delhi-high-court-held-that-prior-foreign-registration-would-amount-to-publication-of-design-india-20251); [Lexology, Delhi HC rules on prior publication of designs](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=c9b43321-f888-40b0-a20a-30ec6b316085)
- **Crocs Inc USA v Bata India & Ors**: Crocs sued on registered design no. 197685 plus passing off. On **9 May 2019 the Deputy Controller of Patents & Designs cancelled Crocs' design registration** on a third party's petition, holding it lacked novelty and had been previously published; the added features "were not sufficient to alter the character or identity of previously published designs known to the art." Prior-publication evidence included the design being available on the Holey Soles website from 2002–2003 — [BananaIP, Crocs–Bata design cancellation costs](https://www.bananaip.com/intellepedia/crocs-bata-design-cancellation-costs/); [C&C IP, Invalidated IP Rights & Cost Liabilities](https://www.candcip.com/single-post/invalidated-ip-rights-cost-liabilities-lessons-from-crocs-v-bata)
- Consequence for Crocs: with the design cancelled, the Delhi HC **disposed of the infringement suit as infructuous (July 2023)** and later **ordered Crocs to pay ₹24.63 lakh in litigation costs to Bata** — [LiveLaw Biz](https://www.livelawbiz.com/amp/design/delhi-high-court-orders-crocs-to-pay-2463-lakh-costs-to-bata-after-design-registration-cancelled-540490); [BananaIP](https://www.bananaip.com/intellepedia/crocs-bata-design-cancellation-costs/)
- Separate Crocs thread (design/trademark overlap): a single judge on **18 February 2019** dismissed six passing-off suits as non-maintainable, reasoning Crocs could not claim common-law trade-dress protection over the *same* features already registered as a design ("dual monopoly"). A **Division Bench (Justices C. Hari Shankar and Ajay Digpaul) set that aside on 1 July 2025**, restoring the passing-off suits and holding a passing-off action for a product registered under the Designs Act is maintainable — [LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/high-court/delhi-high-court/passing-off-action-for-product-registered-under-design-act-maintainable-delhi-high-court-restores-crocs-pleas-against-liberty-bata-relaxo-296204); [BananaIP](https://www.bananaip.com/intellepedia/crocs-passing-off-suit-delhi-high-court-shape-trademark/); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/news/delhi-high-court-crocs-copyright-design-lawsuit-bata-liberty-125070100861_1.html)
- The Supreme Court declined to settle the underlying design/trade-dress overlap question — [Supreme Court Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/journal/crocs-bata-supreme-court-misses-opportunity-to-clarify-a-key-question-of-design-and-trademark-law/)
- **Diageo Brands BV & United Spirits v Great Galleon Ventures**, Delhi HC, CS(COMM) 87/2021: Diageo asserted the registered design of its 180ml "HIPSTER" whisky bottle (smartphone-like flask), claiming novelty in the rectangular shape, smooth rounded shoulders, V-shaped neck, rimmed rounded cap and dimpled bottom. The Court held design piracy must be assessed **from the point of view of an "instructed eye" — a person instructed with the prior art and able to appreciate detail — rejecting the average-customer test** — [SpicyIP, Monopolizing the Hipster?](https://spicyip.com/2022/12/monopolizing-the-hipster-test-of-design-infringement-by-the-delhi-high-court.html); [Mondaq, Design Battle](https://www.mondaq.com/india/trademark/1300102/design-battle-diageo-brands-bv-v-great-galleon-ventures-limited); [judgment PDF via RNA IP](https://rnaip.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/design-case-on-liquor-bottle.pdf)
- **Diageo Brands BV v Alcobrew Distilleries India Pvt Ltd**, Delhi HC, 19 December 2022: a later bottle-design decision that **departed from the Great Galleon reasoning**, indicating the "instructed eye" vs "average consumer" test is not fully settled at Delhi HC level — [Khurana & Khurana analysis](https://khuranaandkhurana.com/2023/01/19/diageo-brands-v-alcobrew-distillerie-why-did-the-court-digress-from-the-judgement-of-the-great-galleon)
- **Whirlpool of India v Videocon Industries**, Bombay HC (2012): Videocon's "Pebble" semi-automatic washing machine closely resembled Whirlpool's registered "Ace" design. The Court found infringement and held Videocon's object was to trade on Whirlpool's goodwill — [LawBhoomi, Designs under Designs Act 2000](https://lawbhoomi.com/designs-under-designs-act/); [Aishwarya Sandeep, Landmark design infringement judgments](https://aishwaryasandeep.in/case-study-of-landmark-judgments-of-design-infringement/)

### Inferences
- **Reckitt Benckiser is the direct threat to Modulark's own conduct**: if Modulark files a design anywhere else first, or if its design appears in any publicly searchable foreign register or public-facing website before the Indian filing date, that is prior publication against itself.
- **Crocs is the cautionary tale**: a registration over a form that is arguably in the prior art is not just worthless in litigation — it is *negative value*. Crocs sued, the registration was cancelled by the Controller, the suit collapsed, and Crocs paid ₹24.63 lakh. For a small manufacturer with a generic cylinder, this is the realistic downside scenario of over-claiming.
- **Diageo's "instructed eye" test cuts both ways for a simple form**: an instructed observer aware of the many existing cylindrical luminaires will discount the cylinder itself and focus on the details — which means (i) Modulark's registration is more likely to survive if it claims details, and (ii) a copycat that changes those details may escape.
- **Crocs (DB, July 2025) is genuinely good news for Modulark**: it confirms that a registered design and a common-law passing-off/trade-dress claim over the same product shape can coexist. So Modulark can build a *second*, longer-lasting layer of protection — shape-based goodwill — that outlives the 15-year design term.

### Gaps
- The Great Galleon / Alcobrew divergence is not resolved by any source found; treat the applicable infringement test as **unsettled** at Delhi HC level.
- Full text of the Whirlpool v Videocon judgment not retrieved (indiankanoon blocked); relied on secondary summaries, which agree on outcome but do not give the quantum of damages.

## (A3) Locarno classification, official fees, timeline, term

### Takeaway
File in **Locarno Class 26, subclass 26-05** (lamps and lighting fixtures). Official fee is **₹1,000 per design** for a startup/small entity/natural person versus **₹4,000** for others; realistic all-in cost with an attorney is roughly **₹8,000–₹25,000 per design**. Expect registration in **3–10 months**. Term is **10 years, extendable by 5 (Section 11)**, 15 years maximum.

### Cited Findings
- **Locarno Class 26 = Lighting apparatus. Subclass 26-05 = "Lamps, standard lamps, chandeliers, wall and ceiling fixtures, lampshades, reflectors, photographic and cinematographic projector lamps."** — [WIPO Locarno list of classes (13th ed.)](https://www.wipo.int/classifications/locarno/its4loc/ITSupport_and_download_area/20210101/revision_files/loc-20210101-E-revision_file.pdf); [WIPO Locarno publication, Class 26](https://locpub.wipo.int/enfr/?class_number=26&lang=en&menulang=en&mode=loc&notion=modifications&version=20190101)
- Official design filing fee: **₹1,000 per design for a natural person, startup or small entity; ₹4,000 for other entities**, under the **Designs (Amendment) Rules, 2021** — [RegisterKaro, Design Registration Fees in India 2026](https://www.registerkaro.in/post/design-registration-fees-in-india)
- **CONFLICT FLAG**: one source reports the small-entity fee as **₹2,000** while stating the natural-person/startup fee is ₹1,000 — [RegisterKaro](https://www.registerkaro.in/post/design-registration-fees-in-india) (self-inconsistent within the same page). The pre-2021 First Schedule had three tiers (natural person ₹1,000 / small entity ₹2,000 / others ₹4,000). Treat ₹1,000 as the likely current figure for a DPIIT-recognised startup but **verify against the current Designs Rules First Schedule on ipindia.gov.in before quoting**.
- Renewal / extension of copyright in design (Form 3, s.11(2)): **₹2,000** for natural person / startup / small entity; **₹8,000** for others — [RegisterKaro](https://www.registerkaro.in/post/design-registration-fees-in-india)
- To claim the reduced fee, a startup or small entity must file **Form 24 with a DPIIT recognition certificate or Udyam certificate at the time of filing**, failing which the ₹4,000 fee applies — [RegisterKaro](https://www.registerkaro.in/post/design-registration-fees-in-india)
- Fee is charged **per design and per class** (Locarno) — separate designs and separate classes are separately chargeable — [RegisterKaro](https://www.registerkaro.in/post/design-registration-fees-in-india)
- Attorney/professional fees typically **₹5,000–₹15,000** per design depending on complexity; flat-fee offerings exist at **₹9,000 with no further fee until acceptance/final disposal**; optional pre-filing design search **₹2,000–₹5,000**; bundled commercial packages advertised from **₹5,999–₹6,999** — [Kanakkupillai, Cost of Design Registration in India](https://www.kanakkupillai.com/learn/how-much-does-it-cost-to-get-design-registration-in-india/); [Bhagnari, Flat attorney fee](https://www.manishmb.com/fee-for-design-registration-in-india.html); [Indium Law](https://www.indiumlaw.com/industrial-design-registration/)
- Timeline: commonly **3–6 months** filing to registration; other practitioners quote **4–10 months**, and packaged services quote **2–12 months**. A **First Examination Report is generally issued within 1–3 months** of filing. The applicant has **six months from the application date to put the application in order for acceptance, extendable by three months on payment of the prescribed fee** — [S.S. Rana & Co., Design Registration and Renewal in India](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/design-law-india/design-registration-india/); [Intepat, Industrial Design Registration in India](https://www.intepat.com/blog/industrial-design-registration-india); [Obhan Mason, Application Stages and Timelines](https://obhanmason.com/blog/application-stages-and-timelines/)
- Term: **copyright in a registered design subsists for 10 years from registration, extendable by a further 5 years on application before expiry under Section 11(2)** — maximum 15 years — [ipindia / Designs Act 2000](https://ipindia.gov.in/the-designs-act-2000); [The Legal School, Features of the Designs Act 2000](https://thelegalschool.in/blog/features-of-design-act-2000)
- Filing requires representations of the design — commonly **four copies of the representation**, with drawings in PDF/JPG for e-filing — [Kanakkupillai](https://www.kanakkupillai.com/learn/how-much-does-it-cost-to-get-design-registration-in-india/)

### Inferences
- **Realistic all-in budget for Modulark: ₹8,000–₹25,000 per design** (₹1,000 official + ₹5,000–₹15,000 attorney + ₹2,000–₹5,000 optional search + drawing preparation). This is cheap enough that cost is not the deciding factor — validity risk is.
- Modulark should consider **more than one design filing**, because the three layers are arguably separable articles: (i) the assembled three-layer module as a whole, (ii) the light head alone, (iii) the wall disc alone. Each is ₹1,000 official. Filing the whole assembly *and* the light head gives two bites: a copycat who changes the wall mount still infringes the head registration.
- **Register as a DPIIT-recognised startup first.** Modulark Tech Labs is a Pvt Ltd company and would otherwise pay ₹4,000/design. Form 24 + DPIIT/Udyam certificate must go in *at the time of filing*, not later.
- Modulark should **not** file abroad before India, or should file India first and use the Paris Convention 6-month design priority window — otherwise Reckitt Benckiser prior-publication logic bites.

- Expedited examination fee for designs (if any currently exists) not confirmed.

## (A4) What design registration actually gets you — Section 22 remedies, and is it worth it

### Takeaway
Section 22 gives statutory compensation capped at **₹25,000 per contravention, maximum ₹50,000 per design** — commercially trivial — *or*, alternatively, an ordinary civil suit for damages plus injunction, which is uncapped. **The injunction, not the damages, is the product you are buying.** The structural weakness is s.22(3): the defendant can attack validity inside the infringement suit on every s.19 cancellation ground, which forces the case into the High Court under s.22(4) and turns a simple infringement action into a validity trial.

### Cited Findings
- Section 22(1) prohibits, during the term of registration, applying or imitating a registered design without the proprietor's consent — [Indian Kanoon, s.22 Designs Act](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1643916/) (via search snippet; page itself blocked); [S.S. Rana, Piracy of Registered Design in India](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/design-law-india/piracy-registered-design-india/)
- **Section 22(2)(a)**: the infringer is liable to pay the registered proprietor a sum **not exceeding ₹25,000 per contravention, recoverable as a contract debt, subject to a cap of ₹50,000 in respect of any one design** — [S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/design-law-india/piracy-registered-design-india/); [The Legal School, Section 22 of the Designs Act](https://thelegalschool.in/blog/section-22-of-design-act); [K&K, What Is Design Piracy Under Section 22](https://ksandk.com/intellectual-property-rights/what-is-design-piracy-section-22-designs-act/)
- **Section 22(2)(b)**: alternatively the proprietor may bring a suit for **recovery of damages and an injunction** against repetition — **no statutory cap**; recovery depends on proven loss. The two remedies are **alternative, not cumulative** — the proprietor must elect one for a given act of piracy — [S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/design-law-india/piracy-registered-design-india/); [The Legal School](https://thelegalschool.in/blog/section-22-of-design-act)
- Suits must be filed in a **court not below the level of a District Court** — [S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/design-law-india/piracy-registered-design-india/)
- **Section 22(3)**: in any suit or proceeding for relief under s.22(2), **every ground on which registration may be cancelled under Section 19 is available as a ground of defence** — including prior publication or prior registration in India, lack of novelty or originality, and that the subject matter is not a "design" within s.2(d) — [The Legal School](https://thelegalschool.in/blog/section-22-of-design-act); [Legal Bites, Piracy of Registered Designs: Grounds of Defence](https://www.legalbites.in/piracy-of-registered-designs)
- **Section 22(4)**: where such a defence is raised, the suit must be **transferred to the High Court** for decision — [The Legal School](https://thelegalschool.in/blog/section-22-of-design-act); [LiveLaw, The Designs Act, Section 22(4) & Interlocutory Powers Question](https://www.livelaw.in/columns/the-designs-act-section-224-interlocutory-powers-question-177406)
- The Supreme Court has interpreted the meaning of "High Court" under s.22(4) — [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2020/12/supreme-court-interprets-high-court-under-section-224-of-the-designs-act-2000.html)
- Injunctions are obtainable in practice where the registration is strong: **Whirlpool v Videocon** — Bombay HC found infringement and that Videocon sought to capitalise on Whirlpool's goodwill — [LawBhoomi](https://lawbhoomi.com/designs-under-designs-act/)
- Injunctions collapse where the registration is weak: **Crocs** — registration cancelled for want of novelty, suit rendered infructuous, ₹24.63 lakh costs against the plaintiff — [LiveLaw Biz](https://www.livelawbiz.com/amp/design/delhi-high-court-orders-crocs-to-pay-2463-lakh-costs-to-bata-after-design-registration-cancelled-540490)

### Inferences
- **The ₹50,000 cap is not a reason to skip registration** — it is simply irrelevant. Nobody sues under s.22(2)(a). The value proposition is (i) an *interim* injunction and (ii) the credibility to make a cease-and-desist letter, a marketplace takedown (Amazon/Flipkart IP complaint), or a customs recordal actually work.
- **The honest cost-benefit for a small manufacturer**: at ~₹10,000–₹25,000 to file, registration is cheap insurance whose main payoff is *extra-judicial* — takedowns and demand letters — because a contested High Court design action in India realistically costs lakhs and runs for years, and s.22(3)+22(4) guarantee the copycat will make it a validity fight.
- **Register, but do not plan to litigate.** Budget the filing as a marketing/enforcement enabler, not as a litigation asset. Only escalate to court against a defendant large enough to be worth suing and against whom the registration is clearly strong.
- **Do not over-claim.** The single biggest determinant of whether the registration is worth anything is whether the statement of novelty survives a s.19/s.22(3) attack. A narrow, detail-specific novelty statement on a generic cylinder is worth more than a broad one.
- Build the **second layer**: after the Crocs Division Bench (July 2025), Modulark can also accumulate shape-based goodwill and later assert passing off / trade dress, which is not time-limited and does not depend on the design registration surviving.

### Gaps
- No reliable published data found on the *typical cost or duration* of a contested design infringement suit in Indian High Courts; the ₹24.63 lakh Crocs costs order is a single data point about *costs awarded*, not about total spend.
- Whether interim injunctions in design cases are granted at a high or low rate in India — no statistical source found.

## (A5) The Copyright Act Section 15(2) trap

### Takeaway
If a design is *capable* of registration under the Designs Act but is not registered, copyright in it **dies automatically once more than 50 articles bearing it have been industrially reproduced**. For a hardware product this means: make your 51st unit without a design registration and you have no design right and no copyright either — the enclosure falls into the public domain as far as IP is concerned.

### Cited Findings
- **Section 15(2), Copyright Act 1957**: copyright in any design **capable of being registered under the Designs Act 2000 but not so registered shall cease as soon as any article to which the design has been applied has been reproduced more than fifty times by an industrial process** by the owner of the copyright or, with his licence, by any other person — [Indian Kanoon, s.15(2) Copyright Act](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/991805/) (via search snippet; page blocked); [Khurana & Khurana, The Intersection of Industrial Design and Copyright Law](https://www.khuranaandkhurana.com/2024/10/09/the-intersection-of-industrial-design-and-copyright-law); [RVR Attorneys, Navigating Section 15](https://www.rvrattorneys.com/navigating-section-15-of-the-copyright-act-understanding-the-boundary-between-copyright-and-design-protection-in-india/)
- Two cumulative conditions must be met for copyright to cease: the design must be **capable of registration** under the Designs Act, **and** an article to which it is applied must have been **reproduced more than fifty times by an industrial process** — [De Penning & De Penning](https://depenning.com/blog/the-synergy-of-copyright-and-design-laws-a-guide-for-indian-creators-ii/)
- Practitioner framing: relying solely on copyright for a commercially produced product design "is a losing strategy in India"; designers must opt for design registration where the shape/pattern/configuration is intended to be applied industrially more than fifty times — [RVR Attorneys](https://www.rvrattorneys.com/navigating-section-15-of-the-copyright-act-understanding-the-boundary-between-copyright-and-design-protection-in-india/); [De Penning & De Penning, Design Registration vs Copyright](https://depenning.com/blog/navigating-dual-protection-when-should-designers-opt-for-design-registration-over-copyright/)
- The Supreme Court has laid down a **"twin test"** for resolving copyright/design conflicts under s.15(2) — [Intepat, Demarcating the Boundaries: The Supreme Court's Twin Test](https://www.intepat.com/blog/demarcating-the-boundaries-the-supreme-courts-twin-test-for-resolving-copyright-and-design-conflicts-under-section-152)
- Recent judicial restatement: artistic works retain copyright protection *unless* industrially applied beyond fifty reproductions without design registration — [Legal Service India](https://www.legalserviceindia.com/legal/article-21015-artistic-works-retain-copyright-protection-unless-industrially-applied-beyond-fifty-reproductions-without-design-registration.html)

### Inferences
- **The trap, stated plainly for Modulark**: the CAD model and the industrial-design renderings of the module are artistic works with automatic copyright from the moment of creation. That copyright is a *decoy*. The moment Modulark manufactures unit no. 51, s.15(2) extinguishes it, because the enclosure shape was "capable of being registered" as a design and was not registered. Modulark then has nothing.
- The 50-unit threshold is **easily crossed by a pilot production run** — a single injection-moulding or sheet-metal batch will exceed it. The trap fires long before commercial scale.
- Practical rule: **design registration must be filed before the first production batch**, and in any event before the 51st unit exists. Since the filing must also precede any public disclosure (s.4(b)), the design filing is the earliest gate in the whole programme.
- Note the asymmetry: the *engineering drawings as drawings* retain copyright against someone who photocopies the drawings; what is lost is copyright against someone who makes the *article*. That residual copyright is close to worthless against a copycat who reverse-engineers the physical product.

### Gaps
- The Supreme Court "twin test" decision is referenced by a practitioner blog but the case name and citation were not obtained. The report-writer should identify it before citing (likely a 2023–2025 SC decision on s.15(2)).

## (B1) Patents Act 1970, Sections 29–33: what destroys novelty and what is saved

### Takeaway
India is a **strict absolute-novelty jurisdiction for patents**. Sections 29–33 are narrow, enumerated *exceptions*, not a general grace period. The 12-month window in Sections 31 and 32 applies only to (i) government-notified exhibitions, (ii) papers read before a learned society, and (iii) public working for reasonable trial. **There is nothing in Indian law resembling the US 35 U.S.C. §102(b)(1) one-year grace period for any disclosure by the inventor.** Since 15 March 2024, claiming the s.31 benefit requires a formal Form 31 filing under Rule 29A.

### Cited Findings
- Exceptions to anticipation are set out in **Sections 29 to 33**; a patent shall not be refused, revoked or invalidated by reason of circumstances which by virtue of ss.29, 30, 31 and 32 do not constitute anticipation — [Mondaq, What Are Not Anticipations](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/247416/what-are-not-anticipations-exceptions-provided-under-the-indian-patents-act); [Lexology version](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=666df564-4d32-4314-b85d-74b0f7c5eb0c)
- **Sections 31 and 32 provide a 12-month period**, commonly called the "grace period", for filing after public display or use of the invention — [Kan & Krishme, Grace Period in Indian Patent Law](https://kankrishme.com/grace-period-in-indian-patent-law/); [iPleaders, Anticipation of an Invention in India](https://blog.ipleaders.in/anticipation-invention-india/)
- **Section 31** covers: display of the invention (or of an article made with it) **at an industrial or other exhibition to which the provisions of the section have been extended by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette**; publication of a description of the invention consequent on such display; use of the invention during the exhibition period by a person other than the applicant without consent; and **the description of the invention in a paper read by the true and first inventor before a learned society, or published with his consent in the transactions of such a society** — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/247416/what-are-not-anticipations-exceptions-provided-under-the-indian-patents-act); [Chambers & Partners, Grace Period Exemptions on Scientific Publications](https://chambers.com/articles/grace-period-exemptions-on-scientific-publications-under-the-indian-patent-law)
- The **"learned society" and "transactions" limbs are not defined** in Indian patent law and are "susceptible to varied interpretation by Patent Offices" — [Chambers & Partners](https://chambers.com/articles/grace-period-exemptions-on-scientific-publications-under-the-indian-patent-law); [Kan & Krishme](https://kankrishme.com/grace-period-in-indian-patent-law/)
- **Section 32 (anticipation by public working)**: an invention is not anticipated by **public working in India within one year before the priority date** by the applicant or a person deriving title from him, **provided** the working was effected **for the purpose of reasonable trial only**, and having regard to the nature of the invention it was necessary that the working be in public — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/247416/what-are-not-anticipations-exceptions-provided-under-the-indian-patents-act); [Fox Mandal, Anticipation of Invention](https://foxmandal.in/anticipation-of-invention-patent-and-latent-threats/)
- **Section 30**: communication of the invention to the Government or to a person authorised by the Government to investigate it is not an anticipation. **Section 29**: prior publication in a specification filed in pursuance of an application made in India before 1 January 1912, or publication obtained/made without consent in certain circumstances, is not an anticipation. **Section 33**: use and publication after a provisional specification is filed / after an application in a convention country — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/247416/what-are-not-anticipations-exceptions-provided-under-the-indian-patents-act)
- **Patents (Amendment) Rules 2024, effective 15 March 2024**, introduced **Rule 29A and Form 31**: "An application to avail the period specified under section 31 shall be filed in Form 31, along with the fees specified in the First Schedule." The applicant must supply details of the prior disclosure and supporting evidence — [iPleaders](https://blog.ipleaders.in/anticipation-invention-india/); [Abhijit Bhand, Form 31: Grace period](https://abhijitbhand.com/blog/form-31-grace-period); [Kan & Krishme](https://kankrishme.com/grace-period-in-indian-patent-law/)
- Section 31 of the Indian Act is **largely identical to the UK provision**, except that the UK grace period is six months from the exhibition/disclosure while India gives twelve — [Kan & Krishme](https://kankrishme.com/grace-period-in-indian-patent-law/)
- Practitioner assessment: these safeguards are "narrow and hard to rely on" — [Kan & Krishme](https://kankrishme.com/grace-period-in-indian-patent-law/); see also [RK Dewan, Patent After Public Disclosure in India](https://www.rkdewan.com/blogs/patent-after-public-disclosure-in-india/)

### Inferences
- **The critical negative finding, stated unambiguously**: an inventor's own voluntary disclosure — an Instagram post, a press interview, a Kickstarter page, a public GitHub repo, a demo at a non-notified trade show — is **NOT** within Sections 29–33 and **destroys Indian patent novelty immediately and permanently**. There is no cure.
- The exhibition limb of s.31 is close to useless in practice, because it requires the *specific exhibition* to have been **notified by the Central Government in the Official Gazette**. Ordinary commercial trade fairs (Light India, ACETECH, Index, a CES-style event) are not automatically covered. Modulark must not assume a trade show qualifies.
- The "learned society" limb is aimed at academic publication, not at product launches. It is irrelevant to a lighting startup.
- **Section 32 is the only limb that might help Modulark**, and only for genuine pilot installations: installing prototypes in a real building to test thermal behaviour, battery life, or mounting could be "public working for reasonable trial." But it requires the working to be *in India*, within one year of the priority date, and *necessary* to be public given the invention's nature. Selling units, or installing them for commercial rather than test purposes, is outside it. Do not rely on this.
- Even where a limb technically applies, **Form 31 must now be filed** (post-March 2024), which means the grace period is no longer self-executing — it is an affirmative claim the applicant must make and evidence.

### Gaps
- No public list found of which exhibitions the Central Government has actually notified under s.31(a) in recent years. This is a material practical gap: it is unclear whether *any* current Indian lighting trade fair is notified.
- No Indian case law found applying s.32's "reasonable trial" exception to a consumer hardware pilot deployment.
- Form 31 official fee not confirmed from a primary source.

## (B2) Designs Act Section 4(b) and Section 21: India is effectively an absolute-novelty jurisdiction for designs

### Takeaway
**Confirmed: India has no general grace period for designs.** Section 4(b) kills novelty on any prior disclosure "anywhere in India or in any other country, by publication in tangible form or by use or in any other way." The only escape is Section 21 — a six-month window for exhibition at a **Central-Government-notified** exhibition, and only if **prior notice was given to the Controller**. A 12-month general grace period has been *proposed* but is **not law as of September 2026**.

### Cited Findings
- **Section 4(b)**: a design shall not be registered which "has been disclosed to the public anywhere in India or in any other country by publication in tangible form or by use or in any other way prior to the filing date, or where applicable, the priority date" — [ipindia / Designs Act 2000](https://ipindia.gov.in/the-designs-act-2000); [iPleaders](https://blog.ipleaders.in/mode-and-manner-of-registration-under-designs-act-2000/)
- **Section 21**: exhibition of a design, or of an article to which a design is applied, **at an industrial or other exhibition to which the provisions of the section have been extended by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette**, or publication of a description of the design, shall not prevent registration — [Indian Kanoon, s.21 Designs Act](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1528876/) (via search snippet; blocked); [Lawgist, Section 21](https://lawgist.in/designs-act/21)
- The s.21 exemption applies only if **the exhibitor gives the Controller previous notice in the prescribed form** AND **the application is made within six months from the date of first exhibiting the design/article or first publishing a description of it** — [Lawgist](https://lawgist.in/designs-act/21); [Intepat, Novelty and Originality of Industrial Design](https://www.intepat.com/blog/novelty-and-originality-of-design)
- Practitioner confirmation of narrowness: "The grace period available in India is quite restrictive. The grace period is available only if: the design is disclosed, used or published in an exhibition notified by the government; the exhibitor gives prior notice to the Controller of Designs; and the application is filed within six months of the first exhibition." — [Mondaq, Protecting Designs – An Indian Perspective](https://www.mondaq.com/india/trademark/842144/protecting-designs-an-indian-perspective); [Lexology mirror](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b9ddadda-e9b2-4e71-bf47-5878bfe4bcda)
- Comparative framing: countries with this structure "operate essentially as absolute novelty jurisdictions" — [Lexology, Disclosing Before Filing? Grace Period Disharmony](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=46619cc6-e211-4bc6-b78c-3e1f2ab4f310)
- **Reckitt Benckiser** confirms that a foreign registration whose records are public amounts to prior publication destroying Indian novelty — [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2013/03/full-bench-delhi-hc-design-act-reckitt.html)
- **Crocs** confirms that a *website* showing the design (Holey Soles, 2002–2003) counts as prior publication sufficient to cancel a registration — [C&C IP](https://www.candcip.com/single-post/invalidated-ip-rights-cost-liabilities-lessons-from-crocs-v-bata)

### Cited Findings — proposed reform (FLAG: not law)
- On **23 January 2026** DPIIT released a **Concept Note proposing comprehensive amendments to the Designs Act 2000** — [DPIIT Concept Note PDF](https://www.dpiit.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/01/791a71ebde47d93b67560f7394be2fec.pdf); [News on AIR](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/dpiit-proposes-amendments-to-designs-act-2000-to-align-with-global-standards)
- The proposals include a **full 12-month grace period replacing the current exhibition-only exception**, meaning designers who disclose via online launches, investor presentations or market testing would no longer automatically lose novelty; plus **deferred publication up to 30 months**, reinstatement for unintentional lapses, protection for virtual/GUI designs, and a term restructure from 10+5 to **5+5+5 (15-year maximum unchanged)** — [Anand and Anand, Proposed Reforms to India's Design Protection Framework](https://www.anandandanand.com/news-insights/proposed-reforms-to-indias-design-protection-framework/); [Kan & Krishme](https://kankrishme.com/indias-proposed-design-law-reforms-key-amendments-to-the-designs-act-2000/); [EU IP Helpdesk](https://intellectual-property-helpdesk.ec.europa.eu/news-events/news/india-proposes-major-reform-design-law-2026-02-19_en)
- The reforms are also aimed at enabling India's **accession to the Hague Agreement and the Riyadh Design Law Treaty** — [Selvam & Selvam](https://selvams.com/insights/indias-proposed-changes-to-design-law-what-you-should-know/)
- **Public comments closed 22–23 February 2026**; as of the searches conducted, **no amending Act or Rules have been notified** — [Selvam & Selvam](https://selvams.com/insights/indias-proposed-changes-to-design-law-what-you-should-know/); [SpicyIP, Design Law Reforms: Searching for Justifications](https://spicyip.com/2026/02/design-law-reforms-searching-for-justifications.html)

### Inferences
- **Answer to the user's direct question: yes, your belief is correct.** India has essentially **no general grace period for designs**. The only route is s.21, and it requires the exhibition to be Gazette-notified *and* prior notice to the Controller. A founder who discloses first and asks a lawyer afterwards has, in almost every case, already lost the Indian design right permanently.
- The s.21 route is **prospective only** — you must give notice to the Controller *before* exhibiting. It cannot be used retrospectively to rescue a disclosure that has already happened.
- **Do not plan around the proposed 12-month grace period.** It is a January 2026 concept note at consultation stage. Treat it as a possible future improvement, not as a basis for disclosing now. If it is enacted later, it will not retroactively restore novelty already destroyed.
- Because s.4(b) says "anywhere in India or in any other country," **the design is also destroyed by Modulark's own foreign disclosure** — a US Kickstarter page, a European trade show, an international press piece.

### Gaps
- No list located of exhibitions currently notified under s.21 by the Central Government.
- Whether the DPIIT amendments have progressed to a Bill since February 2026 could not be confirmed from any source dated after the consultation close; the report-writer should re-check before publication.

## (B3) Founder scenarios — verdicts

### Takeaway
Only three of the ten scenarios are safe, and all three are safe for the same reason: the disclosure is **confidential**, not public. Everything else destroys both patent and design novelty in India, permanently, with no cure.

### Legal basis relied on (cited above, restated)
- Designs: s.4(b) — public disclosure **anywhere in the world**, by publication in tangible form **or by use or in any other way**, before the filing date — [ipindia](https://ipindia.gov.in/the-designs-act-2000)
- Patents: absolute novelty, with only ss.29–33 exceptions; s.31 needs a **Gazette-notified exhibition**; s.32 needs **reasonable trial** — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/247416/what-are-not-anticipations-exceptions-provided-under-the-indian-patents-act)
- Confidentiality: disclosures made privately under an NDA **generally do not count as public disclosure** and preserve novelty; India has no dedicated trade secrets statute, so confidentiality is enforced through the **Indian Contract Act 1872** and the **equitable doctrine of breach of confidence** — [DigiLawyer, Invention NDA](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda); [Abhijit Bhand, How Can I Keep My Invention Confidential Before Filing a Patent?](https://abhijitbhand.com/blog.php?slug=how-can-i-keep-my-invention-confidential-before-filing-a-patent)
- Practitioner warning specific to India: "a casual demo, a pitch deck forwarded around, even a campus showcase can cost you novelty" — [DigiLawyer](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda)

### Inferences — scenario-by-scenario verdicts

| # | Scenario | Patent novelty (India) | Design novelty (India) | Verdict / what saves it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Working prototype shown to interior designers, **no NDA** | **DESTROYED** | **DESTROYED** | Nothing. This is "use... in any other way" under s.4(b) and an enabling public disclosure for patents. Designers are under no obligation of confidence. Worst-case scenario in the list because founders think a meeting is "private". |
| 2 | Same meeting, **signed NDA** | **SAFE** | **SAFE** | The NDA. The disclosure is made in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence, so it is not "to the public". Keep the signed copy and an attendance record. |
| 3 | Photo of the enclosure on Instagram / X / LinkedIn | **DESTROYED** (if the photo enables the invention) | **DESTROYED** | Nothing. Publication in tangible form, worldwide, dated and archived. Even a single post to a small following counts; there is no *de minimis*. Crocs was cancelled partly on a website from 2002. |
| 4 | Crowdfunding page (Kickstarter / Indiegogo / Ketto) with renders and specs | **DESTROYED** | **DESTROYED** | Nothing. This is the single most destructive item on the list: renders disclose the design, specs disclose the invention, and the page is permanently archived and timestamped. Crowdfunding **must** come after filing. |
| 5 | Password-protected page shown to a handful of named investors | **SAFE if properly controlled** | **SAFE if properly controlled** | Access limited to identified persons **who are under an NDA or a clear confidentiality notice**. A password alone is not enough if anyone can request the password or forward it — that makes it available to the public. Log who was given access. |
| 6 | Pitch deck emailed to VCs, **no NDA** | **DESTROYED (high risk)** | **DESTROYED (high risk)** | Nothing reliable. VCs routinely refuse NDAs and forward decks. If the deck contains renders or a section view, treat the design and any patentable mechanism as published. **Mitigation: send a "teaser" deck with no product imagery and no mechanism detail; put images and mechanism behind an NDA-gated data room.** |
| 7 | Demo at a trade show **in India**; **abroad** | **DESTROYED** unless the show is Gazette-notified under s.31(a) *and* the patent application is filed within 12 months | **DESTROYED** unless the show is Gazette-notified under s.21 *and* prior notice was given to the Controller *and* filing follows within 6 months | Same answer for Indian and foreign shows: s.4(b) says "anywhere in... any other country", and s.31/s.21 both require **Indian Central Government notification**, which a foreign show will not have. **Assume no trade show is covered unless you have verified the Gazette notification in advance.** |
| 8 | Public GitHub repo with firmware and enclosure STLs | **DESTROYED** | **DESTROYED** | Nothing. STLs are a tangible publication of the shape. Firmware source publishes any software-implemented method. Git history is timestamped and mirrored — irreversible. Keep the repo private until filing; audit for accidental public forks. |
| 9 | Selling 10 units to friends to test | **Probably DESTROYED**; s.32 "reasonable trial" is the only argument and it is weak once money changes hands | **DESTROYED** | Sale is "use" under s.4(b) — there is no trial exception in the Designs Act at all. For patents, s.32 requires working "for the purpose of reasonable trial only" and that public working be *necessary*; a sale to friends looks commercial. **If you must field-test, do it under written test agreements with confidentiality terms, free of charge, with units returned.** |
| 10 | Press article or podcast describing how the system works | **DESTROYED** (to the extent the description is enabling) | **DESTROYED** (if it shows or describes the appearance) | Nothing. Also uncontrollable — journalists publish what they choose. Embargo agreements are contractual comfort only; if the piece runs early, novelty is gone and you have a damages claim, not a patent. |

- **Cross-cutting inference**: the design right is *more* fragile than the patent right, because the Designs Act has no "enablement" requirement. A photograph that would be too vague to anticipate a patent claim can still be a full disclosure of the design. A single marketing render is fatal to the design and possibly survivable for the patent.
- **Cross-cutting inference**: none of these disclosures can be undone. Deleting the Instagram post, taking down the Kickstarter page, or making the repo private does not restore novelty — the disclosure occurred, and archives (Wayback Machine, Git mirrors, screenshots) make it provable by an opponent in a s.19/s.22(3) cancellation.

### Gaps
- There is no Indian judicial authority found that squarely decides whether a password-protected investor page is "public". Scenario 5 is reasoned from first principles plus general confidentiality doctrine and should be presented as the safest *practice* (NDA + named access + logging), not as a settled legal safe harbour.

## (B4) Is an NDA sufficient? What it must contain, and what happens on breach

### Takeaway
Yes — a properly drafted NDA keeps a disclosure non-public and preserves novelty, because the disclosure is then made in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence rather than "to the public". But an NDA is a *contract*, not a shield against the register: if the recipient breaches it and publishes, the design and patent novelty are gone anyway, and Modulark is left suing for damages. **Section 31 of the Patents Act does not rescue an NDA breach in the general case.**

### Cited Findings
- NDAs in India are governed by the **Indian Contract Act 1872** (ss.10, 14, 23 and 27); they must be lawful and voluntary, and reasonable confidentiality restrictions are permitted notwithstanding s.27 — [DigiLawyer, Invention NDA](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda)
- **India has no dedicated trade secrets statute**; confidentiality is enforced through contract (NDAs) and through the **equitable doctrine of breach of confidence** — [DigiLawyer](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda)
- Disclosures made privately under an NDA generally **do not count as public disclosure**, preserving novelty — [DigiLawyer](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda)
- Indian NDAs typically run **2 to 5 years** depending on the nature of the invention — [DigiLawyer](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda)
- Protectable "Technical Information" in this context expressly includes **designs, prototypes, formulas, and source code** — [DigiLawyer](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda)
- **Patents Act s.29** provides that publication obtained or made **without the consent of** the applicant/inventor, in defined circumstances, is not an anticipation; **s.31(c)** covers use of the invention **during an exhibition period by a person other than the applicant without his consent** — [Mondaq, What Are Not Anticipations](https://www.mondaq.com/india/patent/247416/what-are-not-anticipations-exceptions-provided-under-the-indian-patents-act)
- Practitioner framing of the Indian risk: "confidentiality isn't a nice-to-have; it's the difference between owning your idea and watching someone else run with it" — [DigiLawyer](https://digilawyer.ai/drafting/invention-nda)

### Inferences — what the NDA must contain
1. **Definition of Confidential Information** broad enough to cover the *appearance* as well as the *mechanism*: CAD files, renders, STLs, photographs, physical prototypes, dimensions, the fact of the three-layer architecture, firmware, BOM, supplier names.
2. **Express statement that disclosure is made in confidence and does not constitute publication or public disclosure** for the purposes of any patent or design law, in India or elsewhere. This is the clause that matters for novelty and it is routinely omitted from generic templates.
3. **No-reverse-engineering and no-manufacture clause**, and **no filing** clause — the recipient must not file any patent, design or trademark application derived from the disclosure.
4. **Ownership/no-licence clause** — nothing in the NDA transfers or licenses any IP.
5. **Return or destruction of materials**, including physical prototypes.
6. **Marking/oral-disclosure mechanics** — written material marked confidential; oral disclosures confirmed in writing within a stated number of days (otherwise oral demos fall outside the definition).
7. **Survival period** — for a hardware design, at least until the design and patent applications publish; a fixed 2–5 year term is common but should be drafted to survive until public filing.
8. **Named recipients and permitted-recipient control** — employees/advisers on a need-to-know basis, bound by equivalent terms; no onward disclosure.
9. **Injunctive relief acknowledged** — the recipient agrees damages are inadequate and injunctive relief is available; this materially improves the chance of an interim injunction.
10. **Governing law, jurisdiction and dispute resolution** — Indian law, a named Indian court or arbitral seat.
11. **Liquidated damages or an indemnity**, because proving loss of novelty as a quantified loss is very hard.

### Inferences — what happens on breach
- If the recipient publishes in breach, **novelty is still destroyed as a matter of fact** for the design (s.4(b) is concerned with whether the design was disclosed to the public, not with whose fault it was), and Modulark's remedy is a contract/breach-of-confidence action.
- For **patents**, there is a real but narrow argument: **s.29 and s.31(c)** treat certain disclosures made *without the applicant's consent* as not anticipating. A recipient who publishes in breach of an NDA has published without consent. **Whether this rescues the application in practice is not established by any source found** — it would depend on the precise facts fitting the statutory language, and the applicant would need to file quickly and evidence the breach (and, post-March 2024, likely file **Form 31**).
- For **designs**, no equivalent "without consent" rescue was found in s.4 or s.21 for third-party breach — s.21's without-consent limb is tied to exhibition. **Treat the design as lost.**
- Therefore: **an NDA is necessary but not sufficient.** It preserves novelty only so long as it is honoured. The only disclosure that is safe *regardless of the counterparty's behaviour* is one made after filing.

### Gaps
- No Indian case law found applying s.29 or s.31(c) to rescue novelty after a breach of NDA. This is genuinely untested in the sources reviewed and should be presented as an argument, not a safety net.
- Whether the Designs Act contains any "disclosure without consent" saving beyond s.21 could not be confirmed against the bare act (primary sources blocked).

## (B5) The rule of thumb

### Takeaway
**FILE FIRST, THEN SHOW.** Stated precisely and operationally below.

### Inferences — the rule, in the exact form the founder can follow
> **Nothing that reveals what the product looks like or how it works leaves the company — to anyone who has not signed an NDA — until the design application and the patent provisional are filed and you have the application numbers in hand.**

Unpacked into four operational tests. Before *any* external communication, ask:

1. **Does it show the product?** (photo, render, CAD, STL, drawing, physical unit, video, a slide with a picture) → if the design application is not yet filed, **stop**.
2. **Does it explain how it works?** (the swap mechanism, the contact/power architecture, the battery handover, any control method) → if a patent provisional is not yet filed, **stop**.
3. **Is the recipient under a signed NDA, and is the NDA signed *before* the material is sent?** → if not, **stop**. "I'll send the NDA after" is the same as no NDA.
4. **Could this end up anywhere I cannot delete it?** (social media, crowdfunding, press, a public repo, a public trade-show floor) → **stop, permanently, until filed.** Deletion does not restore novelty.

Supporting operational points:
- **The cheapest gate is the patent provisional**, which secures a priority date and starts a 12-month clock to the complete specification. **The design application must be filed too** — the provisional does not protect appearance.
- **Sequence for Modulark**: (i) freeze the industrial design; (ii) file the Indian design application(s) in Locarno 26-05 and file the patent provisional(s) on the mechanism, on the *same day or the design first*; (iii) obtain application numbers; (iv) only then run the trade show / Kickstarter / press / Instagram programme.
- **Before the 51st unit exists**, the design registration must be on file — otherwise Copyright Act s.15(2) extinguishes the fallback copyright as well.
- **Do not rely on**: the proposed 12-month design grace period (not law); a trade-show exhibition exemption (needs Gazette notification and advance notice to the Controller); s.32 "reasonable trial" (narrow, untested for consumer hardware); or an NDA breach rescue (untested).
- **If a disclosure has already happened**, the position is not necessarily hopeless *abroad* — the EU and UK give 12-month design grace periods and the US gives a 12-month patent grace period — but **India is lost**. That asymmetry is itself a reason to check before assuming the whole programme is dead. (Comparative point drawn from [Lexology, Grace Period Disharmony](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=46619cc6-e211-4bc6-b78c-3e1f2ab4f310); [Mewburn, Grace Periods for Disclosure of a Design](https://www.mewburn.com/law-practice-library/grace-periods-for-disclosure-of-a-design-before-applying-for-a-registered-design-right) — the specific EU/UK/US figures were not independently verified in this research and should be checked before the report asserts them.)

- **Class 42** covers the *services*: cloud hosting, website design, **software-as-a-service (SaaS) delivery**, IT consulting, cybersecurity, data analytics — while Class 9 covers the software *product* — [Vakilsearch, Trademark Class for E-commerce, SaaS, Apps India 2026](https://vakilsearch.com/article/trademark-class-ecommerce-saas-mobile-app-india-2026/)
- The Nice Classification is administered by WIPO; the **12th Edition is effective from January 2026** and is used by India — [BrandAuditor, Nice classification (NCL 12) for India](https://www.brandauditor.ai/blog/nice-classification-guide); [USPTO, Nice Agreement current edition](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/trademark-updates-and-announcements/nice-agreement-current-edition-version-general-remarks)
- Official fee is charged **per class per mark** — [Intepat, Trademark Registration Fees India 2026](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-registration-fees-india)

### Inferences — the recommended filing plan
- **Essential now (house mark "MODULARK"):**
  - **Class 11** — LED luminaires, lighting apparatus and installations, wall-mounted lighting fixtures, modular lighting units. *This is the core class for the hardware.*
  - **Class 9** — rechargeable batteries and battery packs; power supplies; electric sensors; downloadable mobile application software for controlling lighting; electric switches and controls. *Essential because the swappable battery pack is a Class 9 good, not a Class 11 good — the user's instinct is right: **batteries are Class 9, lighting apparatus is Class 11**, and a Class 11-only registration would leave the battery pack unprotected.*
  - **Class 42** — SaaS; design and development of computer software. *Essential to cover the existing Modulus school-management business and any lighting-control cloud service under the house mark.*
- **Optional / second wave:**
  - **Class 35** — retail and wholesale services, online retail, business management. Worth it only once Modulark sells through its own storefront or appoints distributors under the brand. It is also a common defensive filing to block squatters.
  - **Class 37** — installation, maintenance and repair services — worth considering if Modulark will install fixtures commercially.
  - **Class 7** — **not relevant**. Class 7 is machines and machine tools; a lighting module does not belong there.
- **Product names** (e.g. a name for the module itself, distinct from the house mark): file in **Class 11 and Class 9** only. Product names do not need Class 42.
- **Budget implication**: as a Pvt Ltd, Modulark is a company — see (C4) for whether it qualifies for the startup/MSME rate. Three essential classes at the reduced rate is ₹13,500; at the full rate ₹27,000, per mark.
- Draft the **specification of goods carefully**: a specification that says only "lighting apparatus" will not cover the battery pack sold as a spare, and will not cover the app.

## (C2) Does existing use of "Modulus" for school SaaS help the lighting business?

### Takeaway
**No — it almost certainly does not help in Class 11.** Trademark rights in India are class-specific and use-specific. "Modulus" in Class 42 for school management software gives Modulark essentially nothing against a third party using a similar mark for luminaires, unless "Modulus" has been declared or is provable as a well-known mark, which on these facts it is not. Worse, "Modulus" and "Modulark" are different marks — reputation in one does not transfer to the other.

### Cited Findings
- **Section 2(1)(zg)** defines a well-known trade mark as one that has become known to a substantial segment of the public which uses the relevant goods/services, such that use of the mark on **other** goods or services would likely indicate a trade connection with the original proprietor — [S.S. Rana, Well Known Trademarks and Law in India](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/trademarks-in-india/well-known-trademarks-india/); [RVR Attorneys](https://www.rvrattorneys.com/protection-of-well-known-trademarks-in-india-legal-framework-and-case-law/)
- **Section 11(6)** sets the criteria the Registrar must consider: extent of public recognition in India and internationally; duration, extent and geographical area of use and promotion; the record of successful enforcement of rights in the mark; and prior registrations — [RegisterKaro, Well-Known Trademarks in India](https://www.registerkaro.in/post/well-known-trademarks); [S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/trademarks-in-india/well-known-trademarks-india/)
- **Section 11(2)** prevents registration of similar marks even for **different** goods/services where the earlier mark is a well-known mark with reputation in India — this **cross-class protection is the defining feature** of well-known status, distinguishing it from ordinary marks — [S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/ip-laws/trademarks-in-india/well-known-trademarks-india/); [Patron Accounting, Well-Known Trademark India: Guide 2026](https://www.patronaccounting.com/blog/well-known-trademark-status-india-section-11-enhanced-protection)
- A mark **need not be registered or even used in India** to qualify as well-known; reputation and recognition alone can support the claim; once recognised, protection extends **across all classes** — [RegisterKaro](https://www.registerkaro.in/post/well-known-trademarks)
- **Section 11(3)** preserves the rights of prior users of **unregistered** marks, who may oppose — and a clean register search does not screen for these — [Intepat, Trademark Search in India (2026)](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-search)

### Inferences
- **Be blunt with the founder: "Modulus" does not help.** Three independent reasons:
  1. **Different class.** Rights are granted per class. A Class 42 SaaS registration does not confer rights over Class 11 luminaires or Class 9 batteries.
  2. **Different mark.** Even if "Modulus" were strong, the lighting brand is "Modulark". Goodwill in MODULUS does not automatically extend to MODULARK; they would have to be shown to be confusingly similar *and* the goodwill would have to reach into lighting.
  3. **Nowhere near well-known status.** The s.11(6) criteria — substantial public recognition, extensive use and promotion, a record of successful enforcement — are met by a small number of marks in India. A school-management SaaS product does not plausibly qualify. Well-known status is the *only* route to genuine cross-class protection and it is not available here.
- **Where "Modulus" *does* help, marginally**: it establishes corporate continuity and a bona fide adoption story (the "Modul-" root is genuinely the company's own), which is useful in answering a Section 11 objection or an opposition arguing bad-faith adoption. It is evidence of honest concurrent adoption, not a right.
- **A real risk the other way**: if a third party owns MODULUS or a similar mark in Class 9 or 11, Modulark's *own* lighting filing could be cited against. The clearance search must cover both MODULARK and MODULUS across Classes 9, 11, 35, 37 and 42.
- **Strategic recommendation**: treat MODULARK as the house mark and file it independently in each needed class. Do not assume any spill-over. If the company wants cross-class comfort, the cheap way to get it is to *file in more classes*, not to argue reputation.

### Gaps
- No search of the Indian trademark register was performed in this session for existing MODULUS or MODULARK marks — the IP India search portal requires an interactive session and could not be queried here. **This is a required next step** (see C3).

## (C3) Is "Modulark" registrable, and how to clear a product name

### Takeaway
"MODULARK" is a coined word and is likely registrable as inherently distinctive — the main risk is **Section 11** (a phonetically or visually similar earlier mark) rather than **Section 9** (descriptiveness), because "Modulark" is not itself a dictionary word. But "Modul-" is a heavily used root in electronics and lighting, so a **phonetic search is mandatory, not optional**, in India.

### Cited Findings
- A **wordmark search** finds identical/similar text; a **phonetic search** identifies trademarks that **sound** similar even if spelled differently (the example given: searching "Klarity" surfaces "Clarity", "Klairity", "Claritee") — [Intepat, Trademark Search in India (2026)](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-search)
- **Under Section 11, phonetic similarity is as strong a ground for refusal as visual similarity** in India — [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-search)
- Phonetic search "catches conflicts that an examiner may cite under Section 11 and that a plain text search could miss" — [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-search)
- The **wordmark, phonetic and Vienna classification searches on tmrsearch.ipindia.gov.in carry no fee**, though the portal may require a one-time verification. Access route: IP India website → **Trade Marks → Public Search** → choose **Wordmark Search / Phonetic Search / Vienna Code Search** — [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-search); [Lexology, How to Use IPIndia Public Search](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=077ec1ae-69f4-4466-9242-dedaa5e7a0fd); [Riva Legal, Free Trademark Public Search Before You File](https://rivalegal.in/blog/free-trademark-public-search-before-you-file/)
- **Vienna code search** applies to figurative/device elements — relevant only once Modulark has a logo — [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=077ec1ae-69f4-4466-9242-dedaa5e7a0fd)
- **Availability ≠ clearance.** Availability asks whether identical or near-identical marks appear on the register. **Clearance** assesses whether the mark can be adopted and used at acceptable legal risk, considering similar marks, **phonetic equivalents, class overlap, prior use, market channels, and likely Registry or third-party objections** — [Intepat, Trademark Search and Clearance Services](https://www.intepat.com/ip-services/trademarks/search-and-clearance)
- **A clean register result does not screen for**: (i) **Section 9** absolute grounds; (ii) **unregistered marks** whose prior-use rights may support opposition under **Section 11(3)**; (iii) **well-known marks** protected under **Section 11(2)** regardless of registration — [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-search)
- A thorough search before filing TM-A reduces the risk of examination objections under **Sections 9 and 11** of the Trade Marks Act 1999 — [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark-objection-reply-examination-report-india)

### Inferences — distinctiveness analysis for MODULARK
- **Section 9 risk: LOW–MODERATE.** MODULARK is an invented word with no dictionary meaning. It is not directly descriptive of luminaires. The theoretical objection is that it is a minor misspelling of "modular", and the product *is* modular — an examiner could argue it is descriptive of a characteristic of the goods under s.9(1)(b). Counter-arguments: the "-ark" suffix is arbitrary, the word as a whole is invented, and the mark is used as a house mark across unrelated classes (42 SaaS) which undercuts descriptiveness. **Expect this objection is possible but answerable.**
- **Section 11 risk: MODERATE–HIGH and unquantified.** "Modul-" is a common root in electronics and lighting. Plausible conflicts to search for: MODULAR, MODULARK, MODULUS, MODULEX, MODULITE, MODULA, MODUL, MODLITE, and phonetic neighbours. **MODULEX in particular is a known lighting-adjacent name and must be checked.** Until a search is run, the risk cannot be graded.
- **Do not skip the phonetic search.** In India the examiner's own conflict search is heavily phonetic; a wordmark-only search will systematically miss what the examiner will cite.

### Inferences — the exact clearance process, in order
1. **IP India public search** at `tmrsearch.ipindia.gov.in` → Trade Marks → Public Search.
   - Run **Wordmark search** with "Start With", "Contains" and "Match With" options, for MODULARK, MODULAR, MODUL, MODULUS.
   - Run **Phonetic search** for MODULARK (and for each product name), which is the search that matters.
   - Run in **each target class separately: 9, 11, 35, 37, 42**. Search results are class-scoped.
   - Also run in classes you are *not* filing in but whose goods are similar — Class 11 conflicts can be cited from Class 9 and vice versa.
2. **Check the Trade Marks Journal** for recently advertised marks not yet reflected as registered, and check pending/opposed applications, not just registered marks.
3. **Check the list of well-known marks** published by the Registry.
4. **MCA company-name check** at the MCA portal (`mca.gov.in`) — name availability / RUN service — to confirm no existing company or LLP is trading under a confusingly similar name. Note: an MCA name approval is **not** a trademark right, and conversely an existing company name can be the basis of a passing-off claim.
5. **Domain availability** — modulark.com / .in / .co.in, plus the product-name domains, and social handles. Register defensively before announcing; domain squatting follows press coverage.
6. **Common-law / market search** — Google, Amazon.in, Flipkart, IndiaMART, lighting trade directories, GST/Udyam listings, Instagram — for unregistered prior users. This is the search that catches **s.11(3)** prior-use oppositions that the register cannot show.
7. **Pay for a professional clearance/availability search when**: (a) the free search surfaces anything phonetically close in Classes 9/11; (b) the name is going onto physical product, packaging, moulds and packaging tooling where a rebrand would be expensive; (c) you are about to spend real money on brand launch; or (d) you plan to file abroad. A professional search adds an opinion on risk, not just a list of hits.

### Gaps

The conflict position for MODULARK in Classes 9, 11, 35, 37, 42 is therefore **unknown** and must be established before the founder commits to tooling or packaging.
- No source found giving typical Indian professional fees for a formal clearance search opinion.

## (C4) Trademark costs, timeline, procedure, term

### Takeaway
**₹4,500 per class per mark** (individual / startup / small enterprise, online) or **₹9,000 per class per mark** (companies, LLPs, others, online) via **Form TM-A**. ## (C5) ™ versus ® in India

### Takeaway
**™ can be used from day one, with no filing at all** — the Trade Marks Act does not mention it. **® may only be used once the mark is actually registered in India**; using it earlier is a false representation under **Section 107** and now carries a civil monetary penalty.

### Cited Findings
- In India, **™ indicates an unregistered trade mark claim; ® indicates a mark officially registered** under the Trade Marks Act 1999 — [Intepat, Difference Between TM and ® Trademark Symbols in India](https://www.intepat.com/blog/difference-r-tm-trademark-symbol); [S.S. Rana, Trade Mark Symbols 101: ™ vs ®](https://ssrana.in/articles/trade-mark-symbols-india/)
- The Trade Marks Act 1999 is **entirely silent on ™** — it does not define it, does not require its use, and attaches no statutory consequence to it. Using ™ is a matter of commercial practice and common-law positioning, not statutory obligation — [CFOmatrix, TM vs ® vs SM: Trademark Symbols Explained India](https://cfomatrix.in/insight/trademarks-ip/trademark-symbols-tm-r-sm-india)
- **Section 107** treats any representation (symbol, word or sign) implying a mark is registered **in India** as legally significant; if false, the person may be liable to a **civil monetary penalty** — [CFOmatrix](https://cfomatrix.in/insight/trademarks-ip/trademark-symbols-tm-r-sm-india); [Mondaq, Trademark Symbols and Registration in India](https://www.mondaq.com/india/trademark/1145500/trademark-symbols-and-registration-in-india)
- Using ® before the certificate issues makes the person liable to a **civil monetary penalty of 0.5% of annual turnover or ₹5,00,000, whichever is less**, under Section 107 — [CFOmatrix](https://cfomatrix.in/insight/trademarks-ip/trademark-symbols-tm-r-sm-india)
- Section 107 applies **beyond just the ® symbol** — it also catches writing "Registered" next to a brand name, using "Regd." as a suffix, or implying registration in any way — [CFOmatrix](https://cfomatrix.in/insight/trademarks-ip/trademark-symbols-tm-r-sm-india)
- Section 107 is **not** engaged where a statement makes clear the mark is registered **elsewhere**, without implying registration in India — [CFOmatrix](https://cfomatrix.in/insight/trademarks-ip/trademark-symbols-tm-r-sm-india)
- Caution on over-reliance on ™ — [India Business Law Journal, "Tread carefully with TM"](https://law.asia/tread-carefully-tm/)

### Inferences
- **Practical rule for Modulark**: use **MODULARK™** on packaging, the website, invoices and datasheets **from launch**. It costs nothing, signals a claim, and supports a later passing-off action by evidencing brand-as-trade-mark use.
- **Switch to MODULARK®** only after the registration certificate issues, and **only in India-facing materials**. Watch out for the reverse trap on exports: if Modulark registers in another country first, ® on Indian-market packaging would still be a s.107 false representation unless the packaging makes the foreign registration explicit.
- Because packaging tooling and moulds are expensive to change, **design the artwork with ™ and plan a later revision to ®**, or use ™ everywhere until Indian registration is complete.
- The **0.5%-of-turnover / ₹5,00,000 penalty framing indicates the penalty was civilised by the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act 2023**, which converted several IP offences into civil penalties. **This should be verified** — the pre-2023 position under s.107 was imprisonment up to 3 years and/or fine.

### Gaps
- The Jan Vishwas 2023 recharacterisation of Section 107 was **inferred from the penalty formula**, not confirmed by a source explicitly naming the amending Act. Verify before the report states it.
- Whether "SM" (service mark) has any recognised status in India was mentioned in one source title but not confirmed in substance.
