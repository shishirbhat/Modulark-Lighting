## (A) Safety standards and mandatory certification

### Takeaway

The lithium pack is squarely inside BIS's Compulsory Registration Scheme under IS 16046 (Part 2):2018 (= IEC 62133-2), and **BIS treats cells and packs as separate products requiring separate registrations** — buying certified cells does not cover the founder's assembled pack. The pack registration obligation lands on whoever manufactures/imports the pack, i.e. the company. Separately, transport is a live operational blocker: lithium batteries are IATA dangerous goods, air-mode domestic courier services largely refuse or restrict loose packs, and the realistic answer is surface-mode shipping only.

### Cited Findings

**IS 16046 / IEC 62133 — the pack standard**

- IS 16046 (Part 2):2018 is identical to IEC 62133-2:2017 and covers secondary cells and batteries containing alkaline or other non-acid electrolytes — lithium systems. Part 1 (IS 16046 Part 1) is the nickel-system counterpart (IEC 62133-1), so **for a lithium product only Part 2 bites** — [InstaCertify IS 16046-2 guide](https://instacertify.com/safety-testing-is-16046-2-2018-lithium-system-cells-batteries); [Standphill India](https://www.standphillindia.in/product/lithium-ion-cells-and-batteries.php)
- "Portable sealed secondary lithium cells and batteries are covered under IS 16046 (Part 2):2018 in MeitY's Electronics and IT Goods (Requirement of Compulsory Registration) Order, 2021 — no manufacture, import or sale in India without a valid BIS CRS registration" and the product must bear the Standard Mark with the R-number — [Standphill India](https://www.standphillindia.in/product/lithium-ion-cells-and-batteries.php); [PCN India Global, Jul 2026](https://pcnindiaglobal.com/2026/07/20/bis-crs-registration-lithium-ion-batteries-india/) (fetch blocked; search extract only)
- IS 16046 Part 2 test content for a **battery** (as opposed to a cell) includes the external short-circuit test: positive and negative terminals bridged through a total external resistance of 80 mΩ ± 20 mΩ, held for 24 h or until case temperature declines — [Amade-Tech IEC 62133-2 test equipment notes](https://www.amade-tech.com/battery-test-equipment-for-iec62133-2/); [BellTestChamber IEC 62133 procedure](https://belltestchamber.com/how-to-perform-iec-62133-testing-on-battery.html)
- BIS registration under CRS is initially valid for two years, with renewal to be applied for at least three months before expiry — [Induce India](https://induceindia.com/bis-registration-for-lithium-ion-batteries-complete-guide/)

**THE DECISIVE QUESTION — does a pack assembled from certified cells need its own BIS registration? Yes.**

- "Battery cells and battery packs shall be considered different products and must be registered separately." — [Standphill India, BIS certification for battery-operated devices / IS 16046](https://www.standphillindia.in/bis-certification-battery-operated-devices-is-16046.php)
- "A pack assembler using registered cells still needs appropriate registration cover for the pack placed on the Indian market… even if you use BIS-certified lithium-ion cells, you cannot bypass the need for separate battery pack registration. While using a BIS-certified cell is mandatory and simplifies the process, the final battery pack must undergo its own separate testing and registration to ensure the assembly is safe." — search synthesis over [Aleph India](https://alephindia.in/blog/bis-certification-for-lithium-ion-batteries.php), [Standphill](https://www.standphillindia.in/product/lithium-ion-cells-and-batteries.php), [IndiaFilings](https://www.indiafilings.com/bis-certification/lithium-ion-batteries), [BL India](https://www.bl-india.com/blogs/bis-crs-registration/bis-crs-registration-for-lithium-ion-batteries)
- A single CRS registration for "Lithium Systems" can cover the different lithium chemistries within that product category, but cells and batteries still go in as separate applications — [Standphill](https://www.standphillindia.in/bis-certification-battery-operated-devices-is-16046.php)
- For a finished battery-powered device: the battery requires CRS registration under IS 16046 (Part 2); the device's own electronics require their own CRS registration under the applicable device standard (historically IS 13252 Part 1, now migrating to IS/IEC 62368-1:2023) — [Standphill](https://www.standphillindia.in/bis-certification-battery-operated-devices-is-16046.php)

**Who holds the registration / imported packs**

- CRS registration is granted to the **manufacturer of the product at a specified factory location**; a foreign manufacturer registers and appoints an Indian Authorised Representative (AIR). Practically this means: if the company assembles the pack in India, the company is the manufacturer and must hold the pack registration for that factory address; if it imports a finished pack, the **foreign pack maker** must hold a CRS registration covering that pack and that factory, and the importer must only import registered, marked goods — [BIS Scheme-II (Registration Scheme) page](https://www.bis.gov.in/product-certification/products-under-compulsory-certification/scheme-ii-registration-scheme/?lang=en); [Induce India](https://induceindia.com/bis-registration-for-lithium-ion-batteries-complete-guide/). **[PARTLY UNVERIFIED IN SESSION — the crsbis.in FAQ PDFs that state the applicant/AIR rules verbatim were egress-blocked; see Gaps.]**
- **Practical consequence (inference, flagged as such below):** importing a pre-assembled pack does not remove the obligation, it *moves* it to the overseas manufacturer. The company's exposure changes from "obtain and maintain a registration and a registered factory" to "verify the supplier's live R-number covers this exact model and factory, and keep import documentation". That is materially cheaper and faster for a low-volume hand-assembly operation.

**2026 regulatory changes that affect the timeline**

Existing licensees must comply by **30 April 2027** (or their licence validity date, if later) — [MPR/Certification-India](https://www.certification-india.com/en/ministry-of-electronics-and-information-technology-meity-introduces-mandatory-rated-capacity-testing-for-portable-lithium-ion-batteries/); [Global Omega](https://globalomega.com/blog/new-meity-order-on-measurement-of-rated-capacity-of-lithium-cells-batteries/); BIS ref doc [guidelines_for_rated_capacity.pdf](https://www.crsbis.in/BIS/app_srv/tdc/gl/docs/guidelines_for_rated_capacity.pdf) (fetch blocked)

**Transport — UN 38.3, UN numbers, and whether it can actually be couriered**

- Manufacturers and subsequent distributors of lithium (and sodium-ion) cells and batteries manufactured after 30 June 2003 must **make available a UN 38.3 test summary** per Subsection 38.3 of the UN Manual of Tests and Criteria. The legal obligation is to *make it available*, not to put a paper copy in every carton — [Compliance Gate UN 38.3 guide](https://www.compliancegate.com/un-38-3-guide/); [IATA Lithium Battery Guidance Document 2026](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/05e6d8742b0047259bf3a700bc9d42b9/lithium-battery-guidance-document.pdf); [Intertek](https://www.intertek.com/batteries/un-38-3-testing/)
- Lithium batteries are classified as Dangerous Goods under the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations because charged battery shipments may overheat and ignite — [FedEx India lithium battery shipping guide](https://www.fedex.com/en-in/shipping-guide/pack/lithium-batteries.html); [DHL India battery shipping guide](https://www.dhl.com/discover/en-in/logistics-advice/import-export-advice/how-to-ship-batteries)
- **[UNVERIFIED IN SESSION — standard DG classification, stated from general knowledge]** UN 3480 = lithium-ion cells/batteries shipped alone (IATA Packing Instruction 965); UN 3481 = lithium-ion batteries *packed with* equipment (PI 966) or *contained in* equipment (PI 967). UN 3480 is the most restricted of the three and is forbidden on passenger aircraft as cargo.
- **Blue Dart:** restricts all IATA Restricted Items and Dangerous Goods. - **India Post:** the Department of Posts Office Memorandum dated **2 May 2021** followed incidents of lithium batteries booked as Speed Post Parcels catching fire or exploding during handling/loading in aircraft. India Post's own position (posted on its official X account) is that **mobile phones and items with inbuilt lithium-ion batteries can be booked via India Post Parcel (Retail) / Registered Parcel** — because these move by surface — while Speed Post is transmitted by air and is therefore restricted — [India Post official X post](https://x.com/IndiaPostOffice/status/1957742383182786578?lang=en); [summary of DoP prohibited items](https://dineshgadhavi.substack.com/p/india-post-prohibited-items-what)

### Inferences

- **The pack needs its own BIS CRS registration.** Certified cells are a *precondition*, not a substitute. The company as pack assembler is the "manufacturer" of a separate CRS product and must apply, test the pack at a BIS-recognised lab, obtain an R-number, and mark it. Expect the usual CRS timeline of roughly 6–10 weeks for lab testing plus BIS grant, and per-model cost in the low-to-mid lakhs once lab fees, rated-capacity testing and consultant fees are counted. **[Cost/timeline figures are an estimate from the compliance-industry sources' general descriptions, not a quoted figure — do not present them as sourced.]**
- **Importing pre-assembled packs is the cheaper compliance path** for a one-person operation: the registration burden sits with the overseas pack manufacturer, and the company's job reduces to verifying a live R-number for the exact model and factory, and keeping the certificate and UN 38.3 summary on file. The trade-off is total dependence on the supplier keeping the registration alive and not changing the pack silently.
- **Assembling packs in-house without a pack registration is an offence, not a grey area.** It means manufacturing and selling a CRS-notified product without registration and without the Standard Mark, which exposes the company to BIS enforcement independent of any consumer claim — and, in a fire case, hands a complainant a ready-made finding of illegality that will colour every other issue.
- **Domestic shipping is workable but only by surface.** The practical rule the founder should adopt: ship module-with-pack-installed or packed-with by surface express (Dart Surfaceline / Dart Apex, Delhivery surface, India Post Registered/Parcel), never book loose spare packs on an air service or Speed Post, state ≤30% state of charge, use UN 3480/3481 marking, and keep the UN 38.3 test summary on file for every cell and pack type. Air-mode booking is the thing that gets consignments seized and penalised.
- **On exposed contacts and "dead until mated":** a test house assessing the pack under IS 16046 Part 2 will apply the external short-circuit test to the pack's accessible terminals regardless of the "dead until mated" claim. The claim is a *design argument*, not a test exemption. What will actually be examined: whether a metal object (the standard probes/jigs, and in practice keys and coins) can bridge the concentric rings; what the PCM/BMS does when it does; whether the protection is redundant (cell-level CID/PTC/vent plus pack-level PCM FETs); whether the FETs are held off by a mating-detection pin rather than by software alone; and whether the pack survives 80 mΩ bridging for 24 h without fire, explosion or leakage. Concentric ring contacts are exactly the geometry that a coin bridges most easily — this should be treated as the single highest-risk design element. **[Inference from the standard's test content and general certification practice; the clause-level text of IEC 62133-2 on accessible terminals was not retrievable — see Gaps.]**

## (B) Liability under the Consumer Protection Act 2019

### Takeaway

Section 84 is strict liability: the founder does not get to argue he was careful. Inadequate warnings are an independent, standalone ground of liability under s.84(1)(e), the s.87 exceptions are narrow and mostly do not help a consumer-product maker, "harm" expressly includes damage to *other* property and mental agony attendant on personal injury, and the criminal provisions (ss.88–91) plus CCPA recall powers reach the individual behind a one-person company. The private limited company is a partial shield for civil damages and close to no shield at all for the criminal and CCPA route.

### Cited Findings

**Section 84 — liability of a product manufacturer**

- "A product manufacturer shall be liable in a product liability action if the product contains a manufacturing defect, is defective in design, has a deviation from manufacturing specifications, does not conform to the express warranty, or **fails to contain adequate instructions of correct usage to prevent any harm or any warning regarding improper or incorrect usage**." — s.84(1)(a)–(e), text per [IBC Laws s.84](https://ibclaw.in/section-84-liability-of-product-manufacturer/) (fetch blocked; search extract); [Lawgist s.84](https://lawgist.in/consumer-protection-act/84)
- **s.84(2): "A product manufacturer shall be liable in a product liability action even if he proves that he was not negligent or fraudulent in making the express warranty of a product."** — [IBC Laws s.84](https://ibclaw.in/section-84-liability-of-product-manufacturer/); [Lawgist s.84](https://lawgist.in/consumer-protection-act/84)
- A manufacturer is not liable for failing to warn about a danger that is obvious or commonly known to users, or which the consumer ought to have known given the characteristics of the product (s.87(3)) — [IBC Laws](https://ibclaw.in/section-87-exceptions-to-product-liability-action/); [KanoonGPT s.87](https://kanoongpt.in/bare-acts/the-consumer-protection-act-2019/section-87-5c530d3a7b90d971)

**Section 87 — exceptions, in full**

- **s.87(1):** a product liability action cannot be brought **against the product seller** if, at the time of harm, the product was misused, altered or modified.
- **s.87(2):** in an action based on failure to provide adequate warnings or instructions, the product manufacturer is **not** liable if —
  - (a) the product was purchased by an **employer for use at the workplace** and the manufacturer had given warnings/instructions to that employer;
  - (b) the product was sold **as a component or material to be used in another product** and necessary warnings were given to the purchaser of the component, but harm was caused by use of the end product;
  - (c) the product was one legally meant to be used or dispensed only **by or under the supervision of an expert** and the manufacturer had employed reasonable means to warn that expert class;
  - (d) the complainant, while using the product, was **under the influence of alcohol or a prescription drug not prescribed** by a medical practitioner.
- **s.87(3):** no liability for failure to instruct or warn about a danger that is **obvious or commonly known** to the user, or which the user ought to have known given the product's characteristics.
— [IBC Laws s.87](https://ibclaw.in/section-87-exceptions-to-product-liability-action/); [KanoonGPT s.87](https://kanoongpt.in/bare-acts/the-consumer-protection-act-2019/section-87-5c530d3a7b90d971); [Indian Kanoon s.87](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/67629439/) (fetch blocked; consistent across three extracts)

**Sections 82, 83, 85, 86 and the definitions — [UNVERIFIED IN SESSION, stated from general legal knowledge; verify against the bare Act]**

- s.82: Chapter VI applies to every claim for compensation under a product liability action by a complainant for harm caused by a defective product manufactured by a product manufacturer, serviced by a product service provider, or sold by a product seller.
- s.83: a product liability action may be brought by a complainant against a **product manufacturer, a product service provider or a product seller**, as the case may be. (Joint-and-several in practice: the consumer can name all three.)
- s.85: a **product service provider** is liable where the service was faulty/imperfect/deficient/inadequate in quality, nature or manner of performance required by law or contract; there was an act of omission/commission/negligence or conscious withholding of information which caused harm; the service provider did not issue adequate instructions or warnings; or the service did not conform to express warranty or contract terms.
- s.86: a **product seller** who is not the manufacturer is nonetheless liable where he exercised substantial control over design/testing/manufacturing/packaging/labelling; altered or modified the product and that was a substantial factor in the harm; made an express warranty independent of the manufacturer's and the product failed to conform to it; the identity of the manufacturer is not known or a judgment against the manufacturer cannot be enforced or the manufacturer is outside Indian jurisdiction; or the seller failed to exercise reasonable care in assembling, inspecting or maintaining the product, or failed to pass on the manufacturer's warnings, and that failure was a proximate cause of the harm.
- s.2(34) "product liability": the responsibility of a product manufacturer or product seller of any product or service to compensate for any harm caused to a consumer by a defective product manufactured or sold, or by deficiency in services relating thereto.
- s.2(10) "defect": any fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard which is required to be maintained by or under any law, or under any contract, express or implied, or as is claimed by the trader in any manner whatsoever.
- s.2(22) "harm" in relation to a product liability action: (i) damage to any property **other than the product itself**; (ii) personal injury, illness or death; (iii) mental agony or emotional distress **attendant to personal injury or illness or damage to property**; (iv) any loss of consortium or services or other loss resulting from such harm — **but does not include** any harm caused to a product itself, or any damage to the property on account of breach of warranty conditions, or any commercial or economic loss including direct, incidental or consequential loss relating thereto.
- s.2(46) "unfair contract" is confirmed by search extract as covering contracts between a manufacturer/trader/service provider and a consumer with terms causing significant change in consumer rights, and contracts involving or implying injury to the person or property of another fall within what makes a contract unfair — [Indian Kanoon s.2(46)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/62183455/) (fetch blocked; search extract)

**CCPA recall and criminal provisions**

- The Central Consumer Protection Authority may pass orders for **recalling goods or withdrawal of services** which are dangerous, hazardous or unsafe, and for **reimbursement of the prices** paid by purchasers — s.20 — [Lexology, offences and penalties under the CPA 2019](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=e644c9ae-68a6-4385-8f89-dfcde0beb54c); [Civilsdaily CCPA explainer](https://www.civilsdaily.com/news/explained-central-consumer-protection-authority-ccpa/)

- **Section 91** (manufacturing/selling/storing/distributing spurious or adulterated goods): imprisonment up to **1 year and fine up to ₹3 lakh** where injury not amounting to grievous hurt is caused; up to **7 years and fine up to ₹5 lakh** for grievous hurt; and for **death of a consumer, not less than 7 years extendable to life imprisonment and fine not less than ₹10 lakh** — [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=e644c9ae-68a6-4385-8f89-dfcde0beb54c); [Jus Corpus](https://www.juscorpus.com/offences-and-penalties-under-the-consumer-protection-act-an-overview/)
- Liability attaches "whether the person acts directly or through any other person on their behalf", closing off the defence that someone else did the actual production or sale — [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=e644c9ae-68a6-4385-8f89-dfcde0beb54c)

Sections 90 and 91 also empower the court to suspend or cancel the licence of the person convicted.
- **[UNVERIFIED IN SESSION]** s.86 of the Act and the CCPA's s.21 power to order discontinuance of false/misleading advertisements, and the offence-by-companies provision that makes every person in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business liable, unless he proves the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence — this is the standard "offences by companies" formulation and is the mechanism by which a sole founder-director is personally prosecutable.

- Indian product liability policies **exclude product recall, product guarantee, and pure financial loss** such as loss of goodwill or loss of market — [BimaKavach, product liability insurance in India](https://www.bimakavach.com/blog/product-liability-insurance-in-india-a-comprehensive-guide/); [New India Assurance product liability policy](https://www.newindia.co.in/product-liability-insurance)
- Premium is rated on turnover, export destinations, coverage limits (AOA/AOY), policy extensions and deductibles — not a flat rate — [BimaKavach](https://www.bimakavach.com/blog/product-liability-insurance-in-india-a-comprehensive-guide/); [PolicyBazaar commercial product liability](https://www.policybazaar.com/commercial-insurance/product-liability-insurance/); [HDFC ERGO](https://www.hdfcergo.com/commercial-insurance/product-liability-insurance-policy)
- Indian insurers writing this line include New India Assurance, HDFC ERGO, Bajaj Allianz and others, and it is sold online to SMEs — [New India Assurance](https://www.newindia.co.in/product-liability-insurance); [Bajaj General Insurance](https://www.bajajgeneralinsurance.com/commercial-insurance/product-liability.html)
- Lithium-ion battery risk is being separately underwritten and loaded by brokers internationally — [Aligned Insurance, insurance for lithium-ion battery risks](https://www.alignedinsurance.com/insurance-for-lithium-ion-battery-risks/)

### Inferences

- **Strict liability, in plain terms for a one-person manufacturer:** under s.84 the complainant does not have to prove negligence. He proves (i) the product was defective or the warnings inadequate, and (ii) harm. "I hand-assembled it carefully", "I used good cells", "I tested every unit" are not defences to s.84(1)(a)–(c). s.84(2) removes even the honest-belief defence on express warranty. The only real battlegrounds left are causation (did *this* pack cause *this* fire) and the s.87 exceptions.
- **The s.87 exceptions are close to useless for this business.** (1) applies only to *sellers*, not the manufacturer. (2)(a) workplace, (2)(b) component-sold-on, (2)(c) expert-dispensed, (2)(d) intoxicated complainant — none fit a consumer lighting product sold direct to the public. That leaves only s.87(3) "obvious or commonly known danger", and a consumer commission will not accept that the specific failure modes of a swappable lithium pack are commonly known to a lay consumer. **Net effect: on a warnings claim the founder is defending on the adequacy of his own labels, not on an exception.**
- **s.84(1)(e) is the ground most likely to be used against this product.** Design and manufacturing defects require expert evidence; inadequate warnings require only holding up the box. A thin instruction leaflet is the cheapest thing to fix and the most expensive thing to have got wrong.
- **What "harm" excluding the product itself actually means in a house fire:** the burnt lamp is not recoverable as harm (it is recoverable, if at all, as ordinary defect/refund relief). The burnt house, furniture, and the occupants' injuries, treatment costs and mental agony attendant to those injuries **are** recoverable. Lost business income is excluded as commercial/economic loss. So the exposure is not the ₹-value of the product — it is the value of the consumer's home and body.
- **Forum:** because pecuniary jurisdiction is set by the *consideration paid*, a consumer who paid a few thousand rupees for a lighting module files in the **District Commission** even if he claims crores for a house fire. That is the worst configuration for a defendant: the cheapest, most accessible, most consumer-sympathetic forum, with a nominal filing fee for the complainant, no requirement for a lawyer, and multi-year timelines during which the founder must keep appearing or keep paying counsel. Defence cost is real even where the claim fails, and is not recoverable in practice.
- **Personal exposure of the founder — be blunt:** the private limited company is a genuine shield for the *civil compensation* award under Chapter VI, provided the founder contracted in the company's name, the product is branded and marked as the company's, and he has not given personal guarantees. It is a weak shield in three specific places. (i) **Criminal:** ss.88–91 offences are prosecuted against the company *and* the persons in charge of and responsible for its business; in a one-person company that is unambiguously him, and the s.91 death provision carries a minimum of 7 years. (ii) **CCPA:** recall, reimbursement and penalty orders are directed at whoever placed the goods on the market and can be enforced against directors for non-compliance. (iii) **Veil-piercing:** where the company is a façade, undercapitalised relative to the risk it creates, or used to evade a liability, Indian courts will lift the veil — an undercapitalised one-man company selling a fire-risk product with no insurance is close to the paradigm case. Add to this that if the pack is sold **without the required BIS registration and Standard Mark**, a complainant has a colourable argument that the goods are "spurious" within the CPA's meaning (goods falsely claimed to be genuine/compliant), which is what unlocks s.91's imprisonment tiers. **[The "spurious" characterisation is an argument, not settled law — the report should present it as a risk, not a certainty.]**
- **Recall cost for a small manufacturer:** the CCPA can order recall *plus reimbursement of the price paid* to all purchasers. For a low-volume hand-assembled product that means refunding 100% of revenue for the affected batch, plus reverse logistics (which for lithium packs must be surface-mode DG-compliant), plus safe disposal through an authorised recycler, plus the cost of tracing buyers — the last being the reason **a serial-numbered sales register matters from unit one**. There is no realistic scenario in which a founder-scale company survives a mandated full recall without the cash having been set aside. And **product recall cost is expressly excluded from standard Indian product liability policies** (see finding above), so insurance will not pay for it.
- **Insurance, practically:** product liability cover is available to Indian SMEs and is cheap at small turnovers relative to the exposure, but (a) the lithium battery exclusion is the thing to hunt for in the wording, (b) recall cost is excluded and needs a separate product recall extension/policy if obtainable at all, and (c) insurers will ask for BIS certification as a condition — an uncertified pack may void cover or be declined at underwriting. **Do not state specific premium figures in the report: no Indian source with actual rate cards for a lithium-battery electronics SME was found in this session (see Gaps).**
- **Case law caveat:** product liability under Chapter VI of the 2019 Act is still developing. There is no substantial body of reported Indian decisions applying ss.82–87 to lithium battery fires as of September 2026 that could be located in this session. Older battery/electrical-fire consumer decisions were decided under the 1986 Act's deficiency-of-service and defect framework, not the strict-liability chapter. The report should say the *statutory* exposure is clear and the *quantum* is unpredictable.

## (C) What packaging, instructions and warranty must say

### Takeaway

Because s.84(1)(e) makes inadequate instructions or warnings a **standalone ground of strict liability**, the instruction leaflet and the labels are not marketing collateral — they are the founder's primary legal defence and the cheapest risk control he can buy. On top of that, four separate labelling regimes apply simultaneously: BIS CRS (Standard Mark + R-number), Battery Waste Management Rules 2022 (crossed-out bin, chemistry, capacity, EPR number), Legal Metrology Packaged Commodities Rules 2011 (MRP, identity, net quantity, complaints contact), and the E-Commerce Rules 2020 if selling direct online. A warranty cannot contract out of personal-injury liability.

### Cited Findings

**Battery Waste Management Rules 2022 — EPR registration and labelling**

- The Rules cover **all types of batteries regardless of chemistry, shape, volume, weight, material composition and use**, across four categories: **Portable**, Automotive, Industrial and EV. A user-swappable consumer pack is a **Portable** battery — [PIB, Government notifies Battery Waste Management Rules 2022](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1854433); [IEA policy record](https://www.iea.org/policies/25166-battery-waste-management-rules-2022)
- **Producers must register with the CPCB** on the portal **www.eprbatterycpcb.in** — application in **Form 1A**, certificate issued in **Form 1B** — and are responsible for collection and recycling/refurbishment of waste batteries against progressively increasing annual targets; producers may do this themselves or authorise another entity — [CPCB EPR portal notice](https://cpcb.nic.in/openpdffile.php?id=UmVwb3J0RmlsZXMvMTUxMF8xNjc5MzkzMzc1X21lZGlhcGhvdG8yNDc1OC5wZGY%3D); [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1854433); [Diligence Certification](https://www.diligencecertification.com/battery-waste-management-rules-2022/)

Batteries containing lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd) or mercury (Hg) must carry the corresponding symbol — [Certification-India, Battery Waste Management and EPR](https://www.certification-india.com/en/battery-waste-management-and-epr/); [NKG guide](https://blog.nkgabc.com/battery-waste-rules-2022/)

### Inferences

**Minimum warning content for the pack, the module and the leaflet (driven by s.84(1)(e))**

The following is the content set a defensible instruction/warning package should carry. It is an inference from the statutory ground of liability plus standard lithium-battery hazard practice, not a reproduction of a mandated Indian list — no single Indian instrument prescribes lithium warning wording for consumer products.

On the pack itself (permanent, not a sticker that rubs off):
- Chemistry (e.g. "Li-ion"), nominal voltage, **lab-verified rated capacity (mAh and Wh)**, date/lot code, model number
- BIS Standard Mark + R-number
- Crossed-out wheeled bin symbol; EPR registration number
- "Do not short circuit. Do not carry loose with keys, coins or other metal objects."
- "Charge only with [exact charger model]."
- Manufacturer name and address

On the carton / in the leaflet, as explicit warnings:
- Do not puncture, crush, drop, disassemble, modify or incinerate
- Charging temperature range, discharge/use temperature range, storage temperature range and recommended storage state of charge — **state actual numbers from the cell datasheet, not "normal temperatures"**
- Use only the supplied/specified charger; do not use a damaged charger or cable
- Do not short the contacts; keep the contact face clean and dry; do not insert metal objects
- Do not carry a spare pack loose in a pocket or bag with metal objects — carry in the supplied case/blanking cap
- Do not insert or remove the pack with wet hands or in wet conditions; observe the ingress rating (state the actual IP rating)
- Stop use immediately and isolate the pack if it **swells, deforms, leaks, discolours, smells, or becomes hot** — place it on a non-combustible surface away from flammables, do not charge it, contact the manufacturer
- What to do in a thermal event: do not use water on an enclosed fire in an unventilated space; evacuate; call emergency services — **state a concrete instruction, not a vague one**
- Keep away from children; small removable pack is a swallowing/handling hazard
- Stop use if the housing, contacts or cable are damaged
- Do not use in series/parallel with other packs, or with third-party packs
- Disposal: do not put in household waste; return to the manufacturer's take-back or an authorised recycler; crossed-out bin symbol with an explanatory line
- Transport: if posting the pack, do not send by air/Speed Post; declare it as a lithium battery

Legal Metrology block on the retail pack: manufacturer/packer/importer name and address, country of origin, net quantity, MRP inclusive of all taxes, month and year of manufacture, and consumer-complaint contact (name, address, phone, email).

**Warranty — what can and cannot be done**

- An express warranty creates *additional* liability under s.84(1)(d), and s.84(2) makes that liability strict even where the warranty was made honestly and non-negligently. **Every promise in the warranty is a new head of liability.** The correct drafting instinct is therefore to promise less, precisely: a defined period, a defined remedy (repair/replace/refund at the manufacturer's option), defined exclusions for misuse/modification/water damage/third-party chargers — and **no performance claims about runtime, cycle life or safety that are not lab-substantiated**.
- **A manufacturer cannot contract out of product liability for personal injury in India.** Chapter VI is a statutory liability owed to the consumer; s.2(46) makes terms that significantly alter consumer rights an "unfair contract" reviewable by the State and National Commissions, which can declare them void; and Indian courts already treat exclusion of liability for negligence in standard-form consumer contracts as substantively unfair. A clause saying "our liability is limited to the purchase price" will be read down or ignored in a personal-injury claim. **[Conclusion is an inference from the sourced findings above; no Indian decision squarely voiding a limitation clause against a CPA 2019 Chapter VI claim was located — see Gaps.]** The practical value of such a clause is confined to *economic* loss disputes, which s.2(22) already excludes from "harm" anyway. So the clause buys almost nothing and, if drafted aggressively, gives the complainant an unfair-contract argument for free.
- Exclusion clauses are also procedurally vulnerable: if the term was not disclosed and provided before purchase, it can be treated as non-existent. If the founder uses one at all, it must be shown at the point of sale, not buried in a leaflet inside a sealed box.

**Returns page for a D2C website**

Must display, at minimum: total price with breakup; country of origin; warranty/guarantee terms; return, refund and exchange terms; delivery and shipment terms; payment terms; grievance officer name, contact and the resolution timeline; and the seller's legal entity name and address. The return policy must not be narrower than the statutory take-back obligation for defective, deficient, late, misdescribed or spurious goods.

**Realistic exposure if a unit causes a fire — what will actually bite**

Heads of claim available to the consumer:
1. Cost of repair/replacement of damaged **property other than the lamp** — building, contents, vehicle (recoverable; s.2(22)(i))
2. Personal injury: medical costs, future treatment, loss of earning capacity, disfigurement (recoverable; s.2(22)(ii))
3. **Mental agony and emotional distress attendant to the injury or property damage** (recoverable; s.2(22)(iii)) — Indian consumer commissions award this routinely and it is the head that most often exceeds expectations
4. Loss of consortium/services (s.2(22)(iv))
5. Refund/replacement of the product itself and litigation costs (as defect relief, not as "harm")
6. In a death case: compensation to the estate/dependants, plus the **criminal** route under s.91 with a 7-year minimum

Not recoverable: the lamp itself, and any commercial or economic loss.

Practical outcome to expect: a District Commission complaint filed for a nominal fee, defended over 2–4 years, with the founder or his counsel appearing repeatedly; an order that turns overwhelmingly on (i) whether he can produce BIS registration for the pack, (ii) whether his warnings covered the failure mode, and (iii) whether he kept unit-level build and test records. A CCPA complaint or a press report of a fire is capable of triggering a recall direction independently of any consumer suit. **[Timeline and outcome pattern is an inference from the structure of the forum system and general Indian consumer-litigation experience; no sourced statistics on consumer-forum disposal times or battery-fire award quantum were obtained in this session.]**
