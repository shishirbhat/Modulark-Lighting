## (A1) Current status and commencement of the DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025, as of September 2026

### Takeaway
The DPDP Rules 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025 (G.S.R. 846(E)), published 14 November 2025, with a three-phase commencement: the Data Protection Board provisions (Act ss.18–26) plus Rules 1, 2 and 17–21 immediately; Consent Manager registration (Rule 4) at 12 months (November 2026); and the entire substantive obligation set (Act ss.3–17 and Rules 3, 5–16, 22–23) at 18 months, i.e. **May 2027**. As of September 2026 the substantive obligations are **NOT yet in force**, and the Data Protection Board — though constituted in law — still has no appointed Chairperson or Members and is non-functional.

### Cited Findings

## (A2) Scope — does the Act reach data that never leaves the device?

### Takeaway
Section 3 applies the Act to digital personal data processed **within India**, irrespective of where the processing hardware sits — there is no "must be transmitted" trigger, so on-device processing is not automatically outside the Act. The decisive question is whether the *manufacturer* is a Data Fiduciary at all: if the firmware runs autonomously on hardware the user owns and the manufacturer receives nothing, the manufacturer is not "determining the purpose and means of processing" in any operative sense, and the s.3(c)(ii) personal-or-domestic-purpose exemption covers the **individual's** processing. **The exemption is worded around the individual, not the manufacturer** — the manufacturer's safety comes from not being a Fiduciary, not from the exemption.

### Cited Findings
- Section 3(c)(ii) of the DPDP Act provides that the Act does **not** apply to "personal data that is processed by an individual for any personal or domestic purpose" — statutory text, s.3(c)(ii), DPDP Act 2023. (Primary text on [MeitY](https://www.meity.gov.in) — could not be fetched here; verify wording.)
- Section 2(i) defines "Data Fiduciary" as any person who **alone or in conjunction with other persons determines the purpose and means of processing** of personal data — statutory text, s.2(i). (Verify against Gazette.)
- Section 2(t) defines "personal data" as "any data about an individual **who is identifiable by or in relation to such data**" — statutory text, s.2(t). (Verify against Gazette.)
- **The DPDP Act has no separate "sensitive personal data" category** — unlike the SPDI Rules 2011, which defined SPDI (passwords, financial, health, biometric, sexual orientation etc.). The DPDP Act treats all personal data uniformly — [Taxmann, DPDP Act vs IT Act](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/dpdp-act-vs-it-act); [Infosek, DPDP vs the IT Rules 2011](https://www.infosek.in/dpdp-vs-it-rules-spdi)

### Inferences
> **These are reasoned legal inferences from statutory structure, not findings from a source. There is no Indian case law, Board decision, MeitY FAQ or published guidance on on-device IoT sensing under the DPDP Act — the Act's substantive provisions are not yet in force and the Board is unstaffed, so no authority exists. Flag this to the founder as genuinely unsettled.**
- **The s.3(c)(ii) exemption protects the householder, not the manufacturer.** Read literally it exempts "personal data processed **by an individual** for any personal or domestic purpose". A private limited company is not "an individual". So if the company were found to be processing, the exemption on its face would not rescue it. This is a narrower construction than GDPR Art. 2(2)(c), whose household exemption is similarly individual-facing — and the CJEU (*Ryneš*, C-212/13; *Buivids*, C-345/17) read it narrowly. India's provision has not been judicially construed at all.
- **The better argument is anterior: the manufacturer is not a Data Fiduciary.** Section 2(i) turns on *determining the purpose and means of processing*. Where a device the user has bought outright runs firmware autonomously, the manufacturer designed the means but does not determine the purpose of any particular processing operation, receives no data, exercises no ongoing control, and cannot access the output. The processing that occurs is the **user's own** processing, for a domestic purpose, using a tool — squarely within s.3(c)(ii) as to the user. The manufacturer's position is analogous to a diary maker, not a diary-hosting service.
- **That argument weakens fast under three conditions**, each of which the founder should treat as a bright line: (1) any telemetry, crash log, analytics or firmware-update beacon that carries device-generated data back; (2) any remote access, cloud pairing, or manufacturer-initiated configuration push that reads device state; (3) any capability that the manufacturer can switch on remotely — because latent control over means, exercisable at will, is arguably determination of means. A firmware OTA channel that only *pushes signed images* and reports nothing is defensible; one that uploads a diagnostic bundle is not.
- **GDPR contrast worth stating in the report:** under GDPR the settled position (EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on controller/processor; and the IoT literature) is that a device manufacturer that receives no data is generally not a controller, but becomes one the moment it specifies purposes for data it obtains. India's Act imports the same "purpose and means" test verbatim, so the same analysis transfers — but with no Indian authority to lean on, and with the added problem that India's exemption is drafted only for individuals.

### Gaps
- **No Indian authority whatsoever** on whether an IoT device maker is a Data Fiduciary for on-device processing. No MeitY FAQ, no Board order, no case law. This is the single largest gap and must be presented as such.
- Whether MeitY has published any guidance note on IoT / edge processing could not be confirmed (meity.gov.in unfetchable).
- The full text of s.3 (extra-territorial application limb, s.3(b)) could not be verified from the Gazette; the commonly-stated position is that the Act applies to digital personal data processed in India, and to processing outside India where connected to offering goods or services to Data Principals in India.

## (A3) Is illuminance / colour-temperature / coarse-spatial-light data "personal data"?

### Takeaway
On its own, a lux reading is not personal data. Held on a device inside one identified household, as a continuous time series that reveals occupancy, sleep and movement patterns, and learned from that specific household's overrides, it very plausibly **is** personal data under s.2(t) — data "about an individual who is identifiable **by or in relation to** such data". The "in relation to" limb is broad and is the phrase that captures this.

### Cited Findings
- Section 2(t): personal data is "any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to such data" — statutory text, s.2(t). (Verify wording against Gazette; could not be fetched.)
- The DPDP Act contains **no sensitive-personal-data classification**, so there is no heightened tier for behavioural/occupancy inference; all personal data attracts the same obligation set — [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/dpdp-act-vs-it-act); [Infosek](https://www.infosek.in/dpdp-vs-it-rules-spdi)

### Inferences
- The absence of a sensitive category cuts **both ways** and should be stated plainly: there is no extra compliance tier to fall into, but equally no argument that occupancy inference is "merely" low-sensitivity. The Schedule penalties are the same whatever the data is.
- The learned-habit model is the sharper exposure than the raw lux stream. A model fitted to one household's overrides encodes when that household is home, awake, and where it moves — that is behavioural data about identifiable individuals the moment it is associated with a purchaser record. Kept strictly on-device and never joined to the warranty database, the link is never made.
- **Architectural consequence:** the company should ensure the device has **no persistent identifier that can be joined to the purchase record** — no serial number readable over any network interface, no MAC-address-to-customer mapping retained. If the only link between "household X" and "device Y" exists on a warranty spreadsheet and nothing flows from the device, no personal data is ever created at the company's end.

### Gaps
- No Indian guidance on whether inferred/derived behavioural data from ambient sensors is personal data. The reasoning above is by construction of s.2(t) and analogy to EU smart-meter jurisprudence (Article 29 WP Opinion 12/2011 on smart metering), not Indian authority.

## (A4) If the design holds (nothing transmitted) — what still applies?

### Takeaway
Plenty. The sensor design is irrelevant to the obligations that arise from **selling to a person**: warranty and purchase registration, support tickets, marketing lists, payment records and courier data are all digital personal data processed by the company as Data Fiduciary, and all of it falls within the Act from May 2027. Separately, the SPDI Rules 2011 under s.43A of the IT Act remain in force **until the DPDP repeal takes effect in May 2027**, so both regimes currently overlap.

### Cited Findings
- **Section 44(2) of the DPDP Act omits s.43A of the IT Act 2000 and s.87(2)(ob), and the SPDI Rules 2011 made under it fall away with it** — [Taxmann, DPDP Act vs IT Act](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/dpdp-act-vs-it-act); [Opsio](https://opsiocloud.com/in/knowledge-base/are-spdi-rules-still-in-force/)
- **The repeal takes effect only on the notified date — reported as 13.05.2027.** Until then "an Indian organisation today owes duties under a 2011 rule-set and a 2023 statute at once"; "the DPDP Act replaced the SPDI Rules" is a statement about 2027, not about now — [dpdpact.net, SPDI Rules vs DPDP Act](https://dpdpact.net/dpdp-vs-spdi-rules); [ConsentOS, DPDP Act vs IT Act 2000](https://consentos.in/learn/dpdp-vs-it-act/); [Infosek](https://www.infosek.in/dpdp-vs-it-rules-spdi)
- Note s.44(3) of the DPDP Act also amends the RTI Act 2005 (s.8(1)(j)), which drew political opposition and demands for repeal — [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Fopposition-claims-section-443-of-dpdp-act-destroys-rti-seeks-repeal-3487897). Not relevant to a private company but worth a footnote.

### Inferences
- **Right now (September 2026) the operative privacy law for this company's customer database is still the SPDI Rules 2011**, not the DPDP Act. If the company collects anything in the SPDI "sensitive personal data or information" list (most relevantly **financial information such as payment card / bank details**), SPDI Rule 4 (privacy policy published on the website), Rule 5 (consent in writing, purpose limitation, opt-out, grievance officer named with contact details and one-month redressal) and Rule 8 (reasonable security practices — IS/ISO/IEC 27001 or an approved code) apply **today**. A company that has never published a privacy policy is already non-compliant with the 2011 Rules, independent of DPDP.
- Practical merge: write **one** privacy notice now that satisfies SPDI Rules 4/5 and is already drafted to Rule 3 DPDP format. Appoint **one** grievance officer whose contact is published, which discharges SPDI Rule 5(9) now and DPDP s.13 / Rule 13 later.
- Categories that are in scope regardless of sensor design: purchase/warranty registration (name, address, email, phone), invoice and GST records, payment/refund data, courier and installation address, support tickets and the correspondence in them, RMA records, marketing email/WhatsApp lists, CCTV at premises, and employee/contractor data. **Firmware update telemetry and crash logs are in scope if they exist — the recommendation is that they should not exist.**

### Gaps
- Whether MeitY has notified a **specific date** for the s.44(2) omission separately from the general Rule 3/5–16 commencement could not be verified from the Gazette; sources state 13.05.2027 but this should be confirmed.

## (A5) What changes the moment anything syncs to a server — the full obligation set

### Takeaway
A single server sync flips the company from "arguably out of scope" to a full-blown Data Fiduciary with the entire Chapter II obligation set, a 72-hour breach-report clock, prescribed security controls including one-year log retention, and a Schedule of absolute penalties up to Rs 250 crore. The delta is not incremental; it is the whole statute.

### Cited Findings — obligations
- **Notice (s.5, Rule 3).** The notice must be given in **clear and plain language**, independently understandable of any other information, and must contain at minimum: (i) an **itemised description** of the personal data; (ii) the **specified purpose(s)** and a **specific description of the goods or services to be provided or uses to be enabled** by the processing; (iii) the **communication link** to the Fiduciary's website/app and a description of other means by which the Data Principal may **withdraw consent** (with ease comparable to giving it) and **exercise her rights** under the Act; and the means to **make a complaint to the Board** — [Ikigai Law, A closer look at the DPDP Rules 2025](https://www.ikigailaw.com/article/647/a-closer-look-at-the-dpdp-rules-2025); [Consently, Rule 3 Decoded](https://www.consently.in/blog/dpdp-rule-3-itemized-consent-notice-guide); [Tsaaro](https://tsaaro.com/blogs/dpdp-rules-2025-explained-full-overview-and-practical-summary)
- The notice must be **presented and understandable independently** of any other information made available by the Data Fiduciary — [Consently](https://www.consently.in/blog/dpdp-rule-3-itemized-consent-notice-guide)
- **Security safeguards (s.8(5), Rule 6).** Rule 6 prescribes a **minimum set of technical and organisational controls**, including: **encryption, obfuscation or masking / use of virtual tokens** for personal data (at rest and in transit); **access control** restricting access to authorised persons; **monitoring through logs of access and processing activity**, with **logs and necessary personal data retained for a minimum of one year** to enable detection, investigation and remediation; measures for **continued processing after a breach** (backups); and **contractual imposition** of equivalent safeguards on Data Processors — [Seclore](https://www.seclore.com/fundamentals/dpdp-rules-2025-compliance-guide/); [K&K, DPDP Rule 6](https://ksandk.com/data-protection-and-data-privacy/dpdp-rule-6-and-indias-new-cybersecurity-compliance-standard/); [AMLEGALS](https://amlegals.com/reasonable-security-safeguards-under-dpdpa/)
- **Breach notification (s.8(6), Rule 7) — two-stage.** To **each affected Data Principal: without delay**, describing the nature, extent and timing of the breach, the likely consequences for that individual, the mitigation measures taken, the safety steps the individual should take, and contact details of a person able to answer questions. To the **Board: without delay** with initial details (nature, extent, timing, probable impact), **followed by a detailed report within 72 hours** of becoming aware, covering updated facts and circumstances, reasons, remedial measures, findings as to who caused it, and a summary of the intimations sent to affected Data Principals. **The 72 hours is extendable only on a written request with justification, which the Board may allow in exceptional circumstances.** The clock starts on **awareness**, not on completion of investigation, and runs continuously across weekends and holidays — [dpdprules.org Rule 7](https://dpdprules.org/rules/7); [K&K, DPDP Breach Notification Rules](https://ksandk.com/data-protection-and-data-privacy/dpdp-breach-notification-rules-and-modern-incident-response/); [ConsentOS](https://consentos.in/learn/breach-notification-requirements/); [matters.ai](https://www.matters.ai/article/dpdp-breach-notification)
- **Retention / erasure (s.8(7), Rule 8 + Third Schedule).** The Third Schedule's **specified retention periods bind only three classes of large platform**: an **e-commerce entity with ≥2 crore registered users in India** (3 years from last transaction/login), an **online gaming intermediary with ≥50 lakh** (3 years from last login), and a **social media intermediary with ≥2 crore** (3 years from last login). Where erasure becomes due, the Fiduciary must **inform the Data Principal at least 48 hours before erasure**, unless the Principal logs in or otherwise initiates contact — [Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/digital/breaking-dpdp-final-rules-out-consent-managers-face-tight-eligibility-e-commerce-social-platforms-get-3-year-data-limitbreaking-dpdp-final-rules-out-consent-managers-face-tight-eligibility-e-comm-84204.htm); [Medianama](https://www.medianama.com/2025/11/223-dpdp-rules-2025-data-fiduciary-obligations/); [Seclore](https://www.seclore.com/fundamentals/dpdp-rules-2025-compliance-guide/)
- **Consent Manager registration (Rule 4)** commences at the 12-month mark with tight eligibility conditions — [Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/digital/breaking-dpdp-final-rules-out-consent-managers-face-tight-eligibility-e-commerce-social-platforms-get-3-year-data-limitbreaking-dpdp-final-rules-out-consent-managers-face-tight-eligibility-e-comm-84204.htm); [AZB](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-phased-rollout-and-key-compliance-milestones/)

### Inferences
- **The Third Schedule retention periods do NOT apply to this company** — it is not an e-commerce entity with 2 crore users, an online gaming intermediary, or a social media intermediary. The general s.8(7) obligation (erase when the purpose is no longer served / consent withdrawn, unless retention is required by law) **does** apply. This is a useful, concrete point of relief to give the founder, but the general erase-on-purpose-exhaustion duty is the one that will actually bite on the warranty database.
- **Rule 6's one-year log retention is the sharpest irony for this product.** A company whose entire privacy story is "we log nothing" would, the moment it operates a server holding personal data, be *required* to keep access and processing logs for a year. The "no logs" philosophy is only coherent if it applies to the **device**; the server side, if it ever exists, must log. Do not let the founder conflate them in marketing copy.
- **The delta is architectural, not procedural.** Going from zero-sync to any-sync creates: a notice, a consent capture and withdrawal mechanism of comparable ease, a rights-request intake and response process (ss.11–14: access, correction/completion/erasure, grievance redressal, nomination), a published DPO or contact person, a documented erasure schedule, a breach playbook with a 72-hour clock and pre-drafted templates, an encryption and access-control regime with one-year logging, processor contracts, and — if cross-border — s.16 compliance. For a small hardware company that is a full-time compliance role.

### Gaps — items requested but not verified in this pass
- **Section 6 consent requirements and withdrawal mechanics** (free, specific, informed, unconditional, unambiguous, clear affirmative action; limited to data necessary for the specified purpose; withdrawal as easy as giving; consequences of withdrawal borne by the Principal; Fiduciary must cease processing within a reasonable time) — this is the commonly stated summary of s.6(1)–(6) but was **not verified** against a retrieved source in this pass. Verify before publishing.
- **Section 9 children's data** — the commonly stated position is: **verifiable parental consent required for all Data Principals under 18**, and **prohibition on tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising directed at children**, with Rule 10 prescribing the verification mechanism (checking against reliable identity/age details or a virtual token mapped to same) and Rule 11 / Fourth Schedule listing exempted classes (healthcare, education, childcare, transport safety etc.). **NOT verified in this pass.** India's threshold of 18 is indeed higher than GDPR's 13–16 range. This matters for a home lighting product only if children are Data Principals — they are not, unless a child registers a warranty or the product is marketed to children. **Recommend the report treat s.9 as a low-relevance-but-confirm item.**
- **Section 10 Significant Data Fiduciary** — designation by Central Government based on volume and sensitivity of data, risk to rights, sovereignty/integrity, electoral democracy, security of the State and public order; obligations include appointing a **DPO based in India** answerable to the board, an **independent data auditor**, periodic **Data Protection Impact Assessment** and audit, and algorithmic-software due diligence. Rule 12 adds annual DPIA/audit reporting and localisation of specified data. **NOT verified in this pass.** A small hardware company will not be designated an SDF — this is a low-priority item.
- **Section 16 cross-border transfer and Rule 14** — the general position is that transfer outside India is permitted **except to countries restricted by the Central Government** (a negative/blacklist model, more permissive than GDPR), and Rule 14 imposes additional conditions on making specified personal data available to foreign States/entities. **NOT verified in this pass.** Highly relevant if the founder uses any foreign cloud (AWS Mumbai vs a US SaaS support desk) — must be pinned down.

- **Penalties Schedule amounts — NOT VERIFIED IN THIS PASS.** The figures the user supplied (up to **Rs 250 crore** for failure to take reasonable security safeguards; **Rs 200 crore** for failure to notify a breach; **Rs 200 crore** for children's-data breaches; **Rs 150 crore** for SDF obligations; **Rs 50 crore** residuary; **Rs 10,000** on a Data Principal for breach of duties under s.15) match the widely reported Schedule, and the Rs 200 crore / breach-notification figure is corroborated by [matters.ai](https://www.matters.ai/article/dpdp-breach-notification). **The full Schedule was not independently retrieved — verify each line against the Gazette.** The structural point is verified and important: **the DPDP Schedule sets absolute rupee caps with no turnover-linked alternative, unlike GDPR's 4%-of-global-turnover formula.** For a small private limited company this is far harsher in relative terms — a Rs 250 crore exposure is not proportioned to a startup's revenue, and s.33(2) requires the Board to have regard to the nature, gravity, duration, type of data, repetitiveness, gain/loss, mitigation and proportionality when fixing the amount, which is the only thing standing between a small company and a ruinous figure.

## (A6) Practical — what this company should do now

### Takeaway
Do the cheap, high-leverage things in the eight months before May 2027: write the design decision down, make "no egress" an architectural invariant that is testable, and treat the customer database (not the sensor) as the real compliance object.

### Inferences (recommendations, derived from the findings above)
1. **Write a Data Map now.** One page, two columns: *data that exists on the device* (illuminance, CCT, coarse spatial array, override history, learned model — all volatile or local-flash only) and *data that exists at the company* (warranty registrations, invoices, support tickets, marketing list). Date it, sign it, version it. This is the single most useful artefact if anyone ever asks.
2. **Publish a privacy notice even though the sensor needs none.** It is already required by SPDI Rule 4 today for the customer database, and it is the cheapest possible proof of good faith. Draft it to DPDP Rule 3 shape (itemised description of data, specified purposes, withdrawal link, rights, grievance contact) so it needs no rewrite in 2027.
3. **Appoint and publish a grievance/contact person now.** Discharges SPDI Rule 5(9) today and DPDP s.13 later. Cost: an email alias.
4. **Make "logs nothing" a firmware invariant, not a policy.** Concretely: no persistent write of raw sensor time series to flash (keep only the derived model state); no ring buffer of spatial frames; wear-levelling-aware erase of any scratch region; and no network stack compiled into the production image at all if there is no radio. If there is a radio for local control, bind it to link-local only and ship with no DNS resolver and no outbound socket capability.
5. **User-initiated local diagnostics only.** If a field-support dump is needed, it must be (a) triggered by a physical button or local pairing, (b) written to the user's own device over a local transport, (c) shown to the user before it leaves, and (d) never auto-transmitted. Document this as a design constraint in the firmware spec, because that document is the evidence.
6. **Firmware updates: pull-only, signed, telemetry-free.** An update channel that downloads a signed image and reports nothing but a success/failure code with **no device identifier** keeps the "no egress of personal data" story intact. The moment the updater reports a serial number plus a timestamp, the company holds a record of when a specific household's device was online — that is personal data, and the whole analysis changes.
7. **Keep the device identifier and the customer record unjoined.** Do not store a serial-to-customer mapping unless warranty administration genuinely requires it; if it does, keep it in one place, access-controlled, and erase it when the warranty expires.
8. **Do not let marketing outrun architecture.** Every privacy claim in the advertising becomes a representation under the Consumer Protection Act 2019 as well as a DPDP-adjacent statement. See Part B — "nothing is transmitted" is a factual claim that must be true of the shipped firmware, testable by a third party with a packet capture, and must stay true through every OTA update. Build a regression test that fails the build if the image opens an outbound socket.
9. **Diarise May 2027** and put a one-day counsel review in the budget for Q1 2027. Nothing more is needed before then.

### Gaps
- Whether a voluntary DPDP readiness certification or MeitY-endorsed code of practice exists that a small manufacturer could adopt — not researched.

## (B1) "The sensor never produces an image" — legal framework and honest risk assessment

### Takeaway
This claim is **risky as stated** and should not be used in that absolute form. A multi-zone photodiode array producing a coarse spatial map of where light lands *is* generating a low-resolution two-dimensional array of light intensities — which is a defensible engineering definition of an image. The claim is a negative absolute about a matter of objectively ascertainable fact, which means the company bears the substantiation burden, and a single expert witness willing to say "that array is an image" collapses it. A positive, precise, quantified statement of what the sensor actually does carries a fraction of the risk and communicates more.

### Cited Findings — the legal hooks
- **Section 2(28), Consumer Protection Act 2019** defines "misleading advertisement", in relation to any product or service, as an advertisement which: **(i) falsely describes** such product or service; or **(ii) gives a false guarantee to, or is likely to mislead the consumers as to the nature, substance, quantity or quality** of such product or service; or **(iii) conveys an express or implied representation which, if made by the manufacturer or seller or service provider, would constitute an unfair trade practice**; or **(iv) deliberately conceals important information** — [Section 2(28), CPA 2019, Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/134265065/)
- Note the **"likely to mislead"** standard in limb (ii) — no proof of actual deception or intent is needed, and limb (iv) catches **omission**. — [Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/134265065/)
- **CCPA Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements for Misleading Advertisements, 2022**, notified **9 June 2022** — [Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leaderships/CCPA-releases-guidelines-for-Prevention-of-Misleading-Advertisements-and-Endorsements-for-Misleading-Advertisements-2022); [PIB press release, 9 June 2022](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1832906&reg=48&lang=2)
- **Substantiation duty:** "comparisons, claims and descriptions in advertisements which relate to matters of **objectively ascertainable facts** should be **capable of substantiation**, and the relevant party **could be called upon to produce such substantiation by the CCPA**" — [Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leaderships/CCPA-releases-guidelines-for-Prevention-of-Misleading-Advertisements-and-Endorsements-for-Misleading-Advertisements-2022); [LKS Attorneys](https://www.lkslaw.com/insights/articles/prevention-of-misleading-advertisements-analysis-of-guidelines-issued-by-central-consumer-protection-authority)
- **Duty of manufacturers, service providers, advertising agencies and endorsers** to ensure claims in advertisements are **assessed, substantiated, verified or supported by relevant data** — [Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leaderships/CCPA-releases-guidelines-for-Prevention-of-Misleading-Advertisements-and-Endorsements-for-Misleading-Advertisements-2022); [Acuity Law](https://acuitylaw.co.in/ccpa-guidelines-an-effort-to-regulate-misleading-advertisements/)
- **Disclaimers (Guidelines, disclaimer clause):** a disclaimer **must not contradict the material claim**, must not **withhold information relevant to a claim whose omission would make the ad misleading or hide its commercial intent**, and **must not attempt to correct a misleading claim already made**. It must be in the **same language as the claim**, in a **font matching the claim**, and placed **prominently** — [Obhan & Associates / Lexology, "CCPA Guidelines tighten the noose around misleading ads"](https://www.obhanandassociates.com/blog/ccpa-guidelines-tighten-the-noose-around-misleading-ads/); [LKS Attorneys](https://www.lkslaw.com/insights/articles/prevention-of-misleading-advertisements-analysis-of-guidelines-issued-by-central-consumer-protection-authority)
- **ASCI Code, Chapter I (Truthful and Honest Representations):** "All descriptions, claims and comparisons which relate to **matters of objectively ascertainable fact should be capable of substantiation**, and advertisers and advertising agencies are required to produce such substantiation **as and when called upon** to do so by ASCI." Advertisements "should not distort facts or mislead consumers by means of **implications, omissions, ambiguity or exaggeration**." Where a claim is expressly based on independent research, **its source and date should be indicated in the advertisement** — [ASCI Codes & Guidelines Book (PDF)](https://www.ascionline.in/wp-content/uploads/guidelines/ASCI_Codes_Guidelines_Book.pdf); [ASCI Code page](https://www.ascionline.in/the-asci-code/)
- CCPA is actively enforcing: **325 notices** issued for violation of consumer rights, misleading advertisements and unfair trade practices, with penalties totalling **Rs 1.19 crore** — [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2085748&reg=48&lang=2)

### Inferences — honest risk assessment of "never produces an image"
**This is not an endorsement. The risk is real and the phrasing should change.**
- **"Image" has no statutory definition in Indian consumer law**, so a tribunal would take its ordinary and technical meanings. The ordinary meaning ("a picture you could look at and recognise something in") favours the company. The technical meaning — a spatially sampled two-dimensional array of radiometric values — **does not**. An N-zone photodiode array read out as a grid is, in signal-processing terms, an image of N pixels. A thermopile array of 8×8 pixels is universally called a "thermal image sensor". The company would be arguing that its sensor is not an image sensor because the resolution is low, which is an argument about **degree**, not kind — and the ad states an absolute.
- **The negative absolute is the problem, not the underlying fact.** "Never" invites a single counter-example to destroy the claim. If a firmware engineer ever dumps the zone array to a PNG for debugging, or a reviewer reconstructs a recognisable shadow outline from the zone data, the claim is falsified in a demonstrable, screenshot-able way. Under s.2(28)(ii) that is "likely to mislead as to the nature or substance" of the product; under s.2(28)(i) it "falsely describes" it.
- **The real consumer concern is surveillance, and the claim that actually answers it is different.** The consumer wants to know: can this thing see me, record me, or send anything anywhere? Those are three separate, individually provable propositions. Bundling them into one unprovable metaphysical assertion about "images" is worse marketing *and* worse law.
- **Concealment risk under s.2(28)(iv) runs the other way too.** If the company advertises "never produces an image" while the datasheet elsewhere describes a "coarse spatial map", a regulator could read the pairing as deliberately concealing that the device does spatially resolve the room. The safest posture is to **disclose the spatial sensing affirmatively and quantify its limits** — disclosure is a defence, silence is not.
- **The disclaimer route is closed.** The Guidelines expressly bar using a disclaimer to correct or qualify a claim that is itself misleading, and bar disclaimers that contradict the material claim. So "never produces an image*" with an asterisk explaining that it does produce a 64-zone array would be **worse than useless** — it is precisely the pattern the Guidelines prohibit.

### Specific alternative phrasings, ranked LEAST to MOST risky
*(Ranking is an inference from the s.2(28) limbs, the substantiation duty and the ASCI ambiguity rule, not from a cited source.)*

**Tier 1 — Safe. Positive, quantified, independently verifiable.**
1. **"The sensor measures light in N zones. At that resolution it cannot resolve faces, bodies, text or objects."** — Best available. It is affirmative, discloses the spatial capability rather than hiding it, states a number a tester can check, and the "cannot resolve" limb is substantiable with a resolution calculation plus a lab demonstration.
2. **"This product contains no camera, no microphone and no image sensor. It uses an N-zone photodiode array that measures illuminance and colour temperature."** — Strong, provided "image sensor" is used in the component-class sense and the BOM genuinely contains no CMOS/CCD imager. Pair the negative with the positive description in the same sentence so the negative is bounded.
3. **"No photographs or video are captured or stored at any time."** — Safe if literally true: substantiable by firmware audit and by showing there is no frame buffer, no codec, no image file format in the image.
4. **"No data of any kind leaves the device. There is no radio / no network connection / no app / no account."** — Safe if true and architecturally enforced; independently verifiable by packet capture and by RF scan. **This is the claim that actually sells the product**, and it is the easiest one to prove.

**Tier 2 — Acceptable with care.**
5. **"Does not contain a camera."** — Defensible on component class, but relies on the consumer's definition of "camera" matching the company's. A 64-zone light sensor is not a camera in ordinary usage. Risk is low but it is a bare negative with no positive counterpart, so it invites the follow-up "then what *does* it do?" — answer it in the same breath.
6. **"No camera."** — Same as above, terser, marginally more risk because headline brevity increases the scope for implied representations under s.2(28)(iii).

**Tier 3 — Risky. Do not use without counsel sign-off.**
7. **"No image data leaves the device."** — Superficially conservative but **logically concedes that image data exists**. It also implies, by negative pregnant, that other data might leave. Under the ASCI ambiguity rule this is a poor formulation. Worse than #4, which claims more and proves more easily.
8. **"Never produces an image."** — **The founder's preferred wording. Highest risk of the set.** Absolute, negative, turns on an undefined and technically contestable term, cannot be conclusively substantiated, and is falsifiable by a single demonstration. It also cannot be rescued by a disclaimer.
9. **"Privacy by design — it literally cannot see you."** / **"blind by design"** — Metaphorical superlatives. "Literally" makes an ordinary-meaning claim into an absolute one; if the zone array can distinguish a person crossing the room from an empty room (which it will, because occupancy inference is a stated feature), the claim is false on its face. Avoid.

**Core recommendation to carry into the report: replace the negative absolute with a positive, numeric specification plus a bounded capability statement. "N zones; cannot resolve faces or objects; nothing transmitted" says more, sells better and is provable. "Never produces an image" says less and is not.**

### Evidence the company must hold — the substantiation file
The Guidelines and ASCI Chapter I both require substantiation **producible on demand**, so the file must exist *before* the advertisement runs, not be assembled after a notice arrives. Contents (inference from the substantiation and due-diligence duties cited above):
- **Bill of materials** with the sensor part number and its manufacturer datasheet, showing the device class (photodiode / colour sensor / ambient light sensor, not image sensor).
- **A resolution statement**: number of zones, angular field of view per zone, and the resulting ground-sample distance at typical room distances — plus the arithmetic showing that distance exceeds facial-feature scale by a large margin.
- **A demonstration report**: photographs of the raw zone output rendered as a grid alongside the scene, showing that nothing is identifiable. Ideally produced or witnessed by an independent engineering consultant, with source and date, so the ASCI "source and date" rule can be satisfied if the claim is ever expressed as research-based.
- **Firmware architecture statement**, signed and dated, recording: no frame buffer, no image encoder, no persistent storage of the zone array, and no network egress path. Plus the build-time test that enforces it.
- **A packet-capture / RF-emissions report** substantiating "nothing is transmitted", repeated for **each firmware release** — because the claim must be true of the shipped build, not the prototype.
- **A claims register**: every claim in every ad, the exact wording, the evidence item that supports it, the date approved, and who approved it. This is the artefact that demonstrates due diligence.
- **Version control**: the substantiation file must be re-verified on every hardware revision and every OTA. A claim that was true of v1.0 firmware and false of v1.3 is a misleading advertisement for every unit sold after v1.3.

### Gaps
- **No Indian decision, CCPA order or ASCI ruling was found on whether a low-resolution sensor array constitutes an "image"** or on "no camera" claims in IoT/smart-home products. This appears to be untested in India. The risk assessment above is reasoned, not precedent-backed — say so in the report.

## (B2) "CRI 95" — what it means, what evidence is needed, and the dimming/tuning trap

### Takeaway
CRI (Ra) is a defined, measurable quantity under CIE 13.3-1995, averaged over test colour samples R1–R8 — so "CRI 95" is squarely an "objectively ascertainable fact" requiring substantiation. For a product that **deliberately varies CCT and dims**, an unqualified "CRI 95" is very likely misleading, because CRI is a point measurement at a specific CCT and output level and will not hold across the tuning and dimming range. The claim must be qualified with the conditions under which it is achieved.

### Cited Findings
- **CRI is defined by CIE 13.3-1995**, which is "valid worldwide". **15 test colour samples** are used to compare the test source against a standardised reference source. **Ra (the general colour rendering index) is the mean of the first eight samples, R1–R8.** The extended indices give detail on **saturated colours (R9–R12)**, **skin colour (R13)**, **leaf green (R14)** and **Asian skin tone (R15)** — [XAL, CRI – Colour rendering](https://www.xal.com/en/services/know-how/cri-colour-rendering-index)
- **CIE S 025/E:2015** is the test method standard for LED lamps, LED luminaires and LED modules — [LISUN summary of CIE S 025/E:2015](https://www.lisungroup.com/standards/cie-s-025-e2015-test-method-for-led-lamps-led-luminaires-and-led-modules-and-its-supplement.html)
- **IS 16102 (Part 2): 2012** specifies **performance requirements** for self-ballasted LED lamps for general lighting services (d.c. supplies up to 250 V / a.c. supplies up to 1,000 V at 50 Hz). Tolerances for testing parameters are "applicable as given in respective IEC/Indian Standards." A **tolerance category is assigned according to the MacAdam ellipse size** that includes the chromaticity coordinates — [IS 16102-2 (2012), law.resource.org](https://law.resource.org/pub/in/bis/S05/is.16102.2.2012.pdf)
- **IS 16102 (Part 1) has been revised to :2026.** BIS has issued implementation guidelines for the revised standard, which replaces IS 16102 (Part 1):2012. **After 2 August 2026 the 2012 standard is withdrawn and no licence remains valid unless compliance with the 2026 revision is ensured.** The revised standard includes testing parameters for **CRI, CCT** and photometric properties (luminous flux, efficacy), and adds a new marking requirement for lamps with bulbs unsuitable for water contact — [C-PRAV Group, March 2026](https://c-prav.com/2026/03/15/bis-issues-implementation-guidelines-for-revised-standard-is-16102-part-12026-for-self-ballasted-led-lamps/); [Standphill India](https://www.standphillindia.in/product/bis-registration-self-ballasted-led-lamps-is-16102-2026.php)
- **IS 16102 marking requirements** (Part 1, safety): the **BIS licence number and reference to the Indian Standard** must be displayed visibly with the Standard Mark; and **variety, lot/batch number, date/week of manufacture, model number and the complete address of the manufacturer** must be marked on the product, the packaging, or a label attached to the product — [Aleph India, BIS Guidelines IS 16102 (Part 1)](https://alephindia.in/bis-guidelines-is-16102-part-1.php); [Global Solution](https://www.globalsolution.co.in/bis-certification-self-ballasted-led-lamps-for-general-lighting-services-is-16102-part-1-2012/)
- The **BEE Star Label schedule for LED lamps** exists as a separate labelling regime — [BEE, LED_schedule.pdf](https://www.beestarlabel.com/Content/Files/LED_schedule.pdf) (content not retrieved)
- **IS 16103 (Part 2): 2012** covers LED modules for general lighting — performance requirements — [law.resource.org](https://law.resource.org/pub/in/bis/S05/is.16103.2.2012.pdf)

### Inferences
**On what evidence is needed to claim "CRI 95" in India:**
- Because CRI is objectively ascertainable, the CCPA substantiation duty and ASCI Chapter I both apply directly. The evidence standard the company should hold:
  - A **test report from an NABL-accredited photometric laboratory** (integrating sphere or goniophotometer per **CIE S 025/E:2015**), reporting **Ra and the individual R1–R15 values**, the **measured CCT and chromaticity coordinates**, the **drive conditions** (current, dimming level, ambient temperature) and the **measurement uncertainty**.
  - Testing on a **production-representative sample**, not a golden prototype, and **per SKU, per CCT setpoint and per chromaticity bin** — because CRI varies across LED bins and the company will not be buying a single bin for the life of the product.
  - An **ongoing conformity-of-production regime**: periodic re-test of production samples, with a documented acceptance limit. A claim of "95" backed by one report from 2025 does not substantiate units shipped in 2027.
- **Measurement uncertainty matters and is routinely ignored.** Typical laboratory uncertainty on Ra is on the order of ±1–2 units. A product that measures 95 is, honestly stated, "Ra ≥ 93" or "Ra 95 ± 2". **Claiming the top of the uncertainty band as a bare integer is the classic substantiation failure.** Prefer **"CRI (Ra) ≥ 90"** or **"typically 95"** with the test conditions, over a bare "CRI 95".

**On the dimming and colour-tuning trap — this is the sharpest exposure in the whole product:**
- CRI is a **point measurement at one operating point**. This product is explicitly designed to vary CCT and to dim. Both change the spectral power distribution, and therefore change Ra. In multi-channel tunable luminaires, the channel mix at the extremes of the CCT range (very warm, very cool) is typically **not** the mix that maximises Ra, and CRI commonly falls at the ends of the range. Dimming can shift LED junction temperature and, in PWM-free analogue dimming or in mixed-channel dimming schemes, shift the spectrum as well.
- **"CRI 95" on a tunable dimmable product, unqualified, is therefore very likely a claim that is true at one setpoint and false at others.** Under **s.2(28)(ii)** it is "likely to mislead the consumers as to the... quality" of the product; under **s.2(28)(iv)** it may amount to deliberately concealing important information (namely, that the figure applies only at a stated CCT and full output). Under the **ASCI ambiguity/omission rule** it is equally exposed.
- **Industry norm vs what CCPA would consider misleading — state the gap honestly.** The industry norm is to print a single CRI figure on the box, quoted at nominal CCT and full output, with the conditions buried in a datasheet. That norm is widespread but it is **not a defence**: the Guidelines make the advertiser responsible for substantiating the claim as a consumer would understand it, and a consumer reading "CRI 95" on a tunable lamp will understand it to mean "CRI 95 when I use it", not "CRI 95 at 4000 K at 100% only". The company should assume the norm is a liability the industry has not yet been tested on, rather than safe harbour.
- **Recommended qualification patterns, best first:**
  1. **"CRI (Ra) ≥ 90 across the full 2700–6500 K tuning range and from 10% to 100% output; Ra 95 at 4000 K, 100% output."** — Best. Two claims, each separately substantiable, each with stated conditions, and the weaker guaranteed floor is the one that carries the range.
  2. **"CRI 95 at 4000 K, full output (CIE 13.3, measured per CIE S 025). CRI varies with colour temperature and dimming level — see specification sheet for the full curve."** — Acceptable. Note the cross-reference must **not** function as a disclaimer correcting a misleading headline; the condition must be **in** the claim, same font, same prominence, per the Guidelines' disclaimer rule.
  3. **"CRI 95"** bare, on a tunable dimmable product — **do not use.**
- **Publish the CRI-vs-CCT and CRI-vs-dim-level curves.** This is the single strongest defensive move: it converts the claim from a contestable headline into a disclosed dataset, it satisfies the "does not conceal important information" limb, and it is a genuine differentiator for a premium product. Competitors who print a bare number cannot match it.
- **R9 is worth claiming separately if it is good.** Ra excludes R9 (saturated red), which is where cheap high-CRI LEDs fail. If the product achieves a high R9, state it — "Ra 95, R9 > 90" — because it is a real, substantiable, meaningful differentiator. If R9 is poor, be aware that a reviewer measuring R9 and finding it low will publicly frame "CRI 95" as misleading even though Ra is technically correct.

**On whether Indian standards mandate declaring CRI on the package:**
- **Not established.** The IS 16102 (Part 1) marking requirements found relate to the **Standard Mark, licence number, IS reference, variety, batch, date of manufacture, model and manufacturer address** — CRI is **not** among the marking items identified. CRI appears in IS 16102 as a **tested performance parameter** (and in the 2026 revision explicitly so), which is different from a mandatory package declaration. **Where a manufacturer does declare a value, the declared value becomes the value the product must meet on test** — that is the general structure of performance standards, and it is the practical trap: declaring "95" on the box converts a marketing number into a BIS conformity obligation.
- **Immediate action item flagged by the research:** the company must build to **IS 16102 (Part 1):2026**, not the 2012 edition, because **licences under the 2012 standard ceased to be valid after 2 August 2026** — [C-PRAV](https://c-prav.com/2026/03/15/bis-issues-implementation-guidelines-for-revised-standard-is-16102-part-12026-for-self-ballasted-led-lamps/). This is a live, dated compliance change that post-dates most online guidance and should be verified with BIS directly.

### Gaps
- **The actual CRI marking/declaration clause of IS 16102 (Part 2) could not be read** — the standard text was not retrievable and search returned no clause-level detail on CRI declaration or tolerance. **Counsel/compliance must obtain IS 16102 (Part 1):2026 and IS 16102 (Part 2) from BIS and read the marking clause directly.** Do not rely on the negative finding above.
- **IEC 62717 (LED modules — performance requirements) tolerance provisions for CRI could not be confirmed.** The commonly cited industry position is that the measured Ra may fall a limited number of units below the rated value (often quoted as rated Ra minus 3) — **this figure was NOT verified and must not be published without checking the standard.**
- **Whether this product is even within the scope of IS 16102** is unresolved: IS 16102 covers **self-ballasted LED lamps**; a room-level luminaire system with a separate sensing node may instead fall under **IS 16103** (LED modules), **IS 10322** (luminaires) or the **Electronics and IT Goods (Compulsory Registration) Order** regime. This scoping question should be settled early because it determines the whole BIS certification path. Not researched here.
- **BEE Star Labelling applicability and whether it mandates a CRI declaration** was not determined; the schedule PDF could not be fetched.
- No CCPA order or ASCI ruling specifically on CRI claims in India was found.

## (B3) Penalties for a misleading claim — confirmed figures

### Takeaway
The administrative exposure is Rs 10 lakh rising to Rs 50 lakh for repeat contraventions, plus the power to order the ad discontinued or modified and to ban an endorser. The criminal exposure under s.89 is up to two years' imprisonment and Rs 10 lakh fine, rising to five years and Rs 50 lakh for subsequent convictions. These are modest next to the DPDP Schedule but they attach personally to individuals, which the DPDP penalties do not.

### Cited Findings
- **Section 21, CPA 2019 — CCPA powers:** the CCPA may impose a penalty of **up to Rs 10 lakh** on a manufacturer, service provider or endorser for a false or misleading advertisement; **for every subsequent contravention, up to Rs 50 lakh** — [Nyaaya, Punishments for Consumer Rights Violations](https://nyaaya.org/legal-explainer/punishments-for-consumer-rights-violations/); [Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leaderships/CCPA-releases-guidelines-for-Prevention-of-Misleading-Advertisements-and-Endorsements-for-Misleading-Advertisements-2022); [LKS Attorneys](https://www.lkslaw.com/insights/articles/prevention-of-misleading-advertisements-analysis-of-guidelines-issued-by-central-consumer-protection-authority)
- The CCPA may also **direct discontinuance or modification** of a false or misleading advertisement, and may **prohibit an endorser** from making any endorsement for up to one year (extendable to three years for subsequent contraventions) — [Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leaderships/CCPA-releases-guidelines-for-Prevention-of-Misleading-Advertisements-and-Endorsements-for-Misleading-Advertisements-2022); [Quintess Law](https://quintesslaw.com/updates/ccpa-guidelines-on-misleading-advertisements-and-endorsements/)
- **Section 89, CPA 2019:** any manufacturer or service provider who causes a **false or misleading advertisement** prejudicial to the interest of consumers shall be punished with **imprisonment up to 2 years and fine up to Rs 10 lakh**; for **every subsequent offence, imprisonment up to 5 years and fine up to Rs 50 lakh** — [Vidhi Judicial Academy, ss.88–93 CPA 2019](https://vidhijudicial.com/sec-88-to-93-chapter-vii-(offences-and-penalties)-the-consumer-protection-act,-2019.html); [aaptaxlaw.com, ss.88–93](https://www.aaptaxlaw.com/cpa-2019/section-88-89-90-91-92-93-consumer-protection-act-2019-chapter-vii-offences-and-penalties.html); [Nyaaya](https://nyaaya.org/legal-explainer/punishments-for-consumer-rights-violations/)
- **Section 88, CPA 2019:** failure to comply with a direction of the Central Authority under ss.20 or 21 is punishable with **imprisonment up to 6 months or fine up to Rs 20 lakh, or both** — [Vidhi Judicial Academy](https://vidhijudicial.com/sec-88-to-93-chapter-vii-(offences-and-penalties)-the-consumer-protection-act,-2019.html)
- **Section 2(47), CPA 2019** defines "unfair trade practice", which includes falsely representing that goods are of a particular standard, quality, grade or model — engaged by limb (iii) of the s.2(28) misleading-advertisement definition — [Indian Kanoon, s.2(28)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/134265065/) (s.2(47) text itself not retrieved — verify)
- CCPA enforcement is active: **325 notices** and **Rs 1.19 crore** in penalties reported in one PIB release — [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2085748&reg=48&lang=2)

### Inferences
- **One source conflated s.21 and s.89**, stating that repeat misleading advertising attracts "a penalty up to fifty lakh rupees with imprisonment extending to five years" ([iPleaders](https://blog.ipleaders.in/consumer-protection-act-2019-2/) / [Legal Service India](https://www.legalserviceindia.com/legal/legal/article-4708-consumer-protection-act-2019-restraining-false-and-misleading-advertisements.html)). **That is a blend of the two provisions.** The correct separation is: **s.21 = administrative penalty by CCPA, no imprisonment (Rs 10 lakh / Rs 50 lakh repeat). s.89 = criminal offence, prosecuted, imprisonment plus fine (2 years + Rs 10 lakh / 5 years + Rs 50 lakh repeat).** Flag this so the report does not repeat the conflation.
- **Compare the two regimes for the founder, bluntly:** DPDP penalties are corporate-only, absolute-capped, and enormous (up to Rs 250 crore) but not yet enforceable and with no functioning Board. CPA penalties are smaller but **live today, actively enforced, and s.89 reaches individuals with imprisonment.** For a company at pre-launch, the **advertising claim risk is the nearer and more personal risk**, and the data-protection risk is the larger but more distant one. The founder's attention is currently pointed at the wrong one.
- **The user's stated figures for CPA 2019 are confirmed**: Rs 10 lakh first offence / Rs 50 lakh subsequent under s.21, and 2 years + Rs 10 lakh under s.89. The endorser ban is confirmed. The only correction is that s.21 carries no imprisonment.
